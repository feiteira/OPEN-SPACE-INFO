    From ESA PSS to CCSDS and ECSS: how the packet standards evolved¶

    The packets, frames and services described across this wiki did not start life as CCSDS Blue Books or ECSS standards. Between 1988 and 1994 ESA published four standards in its own PSS (Procedures, Specifications and Standards) series that fixed, for European missions, how telemetry is packetised and framed (ESA PSS-04-106), how telecommands are coded, framed, segmented and acknowledged (ESA PSS-04-107), how an on-board telecommand decoder is built (ESA PSS-04-151) and what the inside of a packet means (ESA PSS-07-101, the first Packet Utilisation Standard).1265064 The PSS documents adapted CCSDS recommendations by keeping or removing options, and all of them were later withdrawn in favour of ECSS standards or CCSDS books.89 Much of what an engineer meets today in the Space Packet, the TM and TC transfer frames, COP-1, the CLTU and the PUS can be read in these documents in an earlier form, sometimes with different sizes and values. This page explains what each PSS standard defined, how its structures map onto today's books, and how ESOC's mission control systems grew up alongside them. The publishers and the full version matrix are on the standards landscape.

    How the four PSS standards were read

    The four PSS documents in this wiki's library are scanned PDFs. Their text comes from optical character recognition (OCR), which merges words and scrambles tables. Every number, field size, bit position, code value and date taken from them on this page was checked against the rendered page images. Footnotes marked "(OCR)" point to pages whose wording was read from the OCR text; "page image checked" marks the pages behind the diagrams and tables. Only short terms and phrases are quoted from them, in quotation marks.

    Before packets¶

    ESA's space link standards were the PSS-04 branch, "Space Data Communications". It had two sub-branches: space link standards and protocols (PSS-04-1XX) and spacecraft data interfaces and protocols (PSS-04-2XX). Their stated purpose was to keep spacecraft TT&C subsystems compatible with ESOC's tracking and data network and ESTEC's satellite check-out facilities.2 The packet standards replaced older pulse code modulation (PCM) standards: PSS-04-106 superseded the PCM Telemetry Standard TTC-A-02, and PSS-04-107 the PCM Telecommand Standard TTC-A-01.529

    Kaufeler's paper on the PUS, which cites the May 1994 issue of PSS-07-101, describes the world they replaced. Telemetry parameters were digitised analogue read-outs and register states sampled at fixed positions in a telemetry format; control used fixed-length telecommand frames carrying simple register loads or switch commands; and the link guaranteed neither reliable nor complete delivery. As more functions moved into on-board software during the 1980s, that hardware-oriented interface became a constraint, and CCSDS recommended packets as the remedy.74

    Both packet standards call themselves a "derived subset" of the CCSDS recommendations. They warn that other agencies were only starting their own CCSDS-based standards, so cross-support had to be negotiated case by case.2449 ESA's choices were therefore narrower than CCSDS's: one packet version, one frame version, one secondary header format and forward packet order only.9161817

    PSS-04-106: the Packet Telemetry Standard (1988)¶

    PSS-04-106 defined three data structures: the Source Packet that an on-board application process hands over, the Telemetry Packet used to segment long source packets, and the Transfer Frame that carries them in fixed-length frames over virtual channels.57 Its bit numbering is the one CCSDS books still use: bit 0 is the first bit transmitted and is the most significant bit of a value.6107

    Source packets and segmentation¶

    A source packet had a 48-bit header and a data field of 1 to 65 536 octets, so a packet was 7 to 65 542 octets long, exactly the limits of today's Space Packet.11108

    The Telemetry Packet was the segment. To stop long source packets from capturing the downlink, any source packet whose data field exceeded a per-virtual-channel limit LSEGMENT was cut into telemetry packets carrying the same header, with the segmentation flags marking first, continuation and last segments. LSEGMENT could be 256, 512 or 1024 octets, or 65 536 for no segmentation, and was signalled in every frame by the 2-bit Segment Length Identifier. In segments the length field did not give the segment's own length but the part of the original data field still to come.81217 The standard's own example cuts a 2106-octet source packet (2100 octets of data) with LSEGMENT = 512 into four 518-octet segments and a 58-octet last segment, with length fields 2099, 2099, 1587, 1075, 563 and 51 across the original and its five pieces.13 Appendix A explains that ESA renamed CCSDS terms: the CCSDS "Version 2 Telemetry Segment" became the ESA "Telemetry Packet", the CCSDS "secondary header" the "data field header", and octets were numbered from 0 rather than from 1.25

    The 48-bit frame primary header of 1988 is field for field the header of CCSDS 132.0-B-3 of 2021: version 2 bits, spacecraft ID 10, virtual channel ID 3, operational control field flag 1, master channel frame count 8, virtual channel frame count 8, and a 16-bit data field status with secondary header flag, synchronisation flag, packet order flag, segment length identifier and first header pointer.16114

    Time, ground processing and services¶

    Spacecraft elapsed time was a free-running counter in the CCSDS unsegmented time code (CUC): a P-field 0b0010xxyy with 1 to 4 octets of coarse time and 0 to 3 octets of fine time, in TAI seconds from an arbitrary epoch. Time correlation sampled the clock at the first bit of the synchronisation marker of the virtual channel 0 frame whose virtual channel count was 0 (or every 2n frames, n = 0 to 8, coded in the first octet of the packet), and sent the sample in a source packet with APID 0 and no data field header. The ground time-tagged the same marker in UTC and derived the correlation.22 (The printed P-field table repeats the value 00100111 where 00100110 would complete the pattern, a misprint visible on the page image.)22 This scheme was later transcribed into Annex C of ECSS-E-70-41A; see ST[09] Time management.97

    The ground was expected to acquire frames, demultiplex virtual channels, extract telemetry packets and rebuild source packets, delivering incomplete ones with their gaps flagged.21 ESA offered two network services: Service 1, source packet delivery per virtual channel with time correlation and CLCW servicing; and Service 2, transfer frame delivery, for frames whose contents were not standard forward-ordered telemetry packets or standard playback frames. A per-virtual-channel form of Service 2 was also what the network itself used to extract asynchronously inserted playback frames.23 How a ground station hands packets and frames to a control centre today is described under SLE.

    Physical and coding layers¶

    The physical layer was a sequence of four carrier modulation modes (CMM-1 unmodulated carrier, CMM-2 acquisition sequence of at least 128 bits, CMM-3 one CLTU, CMM-4 idle sequence of at least 8 bits between CLTUs), applied in the order of ESA's PLOP-2. Two CLCW bits, "No RF Available" (bit 16) and "No Bit Lock" (bit 17), reported the physical link back to the ground.32

    The CLTU (Command Link Transmission Unit) began with the 16-bit start sequence 0xEB90, followed by codeblocks and a tail sequence one codeblock long, made of alternating zeros and ones and ending with a one.33 Each codeblock carried 4, 5, 6 or 7 information octets (the 8-octet codeblock, with 7 information octets, was recommended) plus one octet holding seven complemented parity bits of a (63,56) modified BCH code with generator \(g(x)=x^7+x^6+x^2+1\) and a filler bit of 0. Shortened codeblocks were encoded as if preceded by zero "virtual fill".3334 Decoding corrected single errors, and the filler bit let the decoder reliably reject the tail sequence.35 CCSDS 231.0-B-4 keeps the start sequence, the code and the complemented parity, but defines only the 8-octet codeword, a different tail pattern (0xC5C5C5C5C5C5C579) and LDPC options; ECSS-E-ST-50-04C lists the dropped 56-, 48- and 40-bit codeblocks, the changed tail and the added randomisation among its changes from PSS-04-107.12712893

    The transfer frame and COP-1¶

    The TC frame of 1992 had a 5-octet header, 1 to 249 octets of data and a mandatory 2-octet CRC, for 8 to 256 octets in all. The bypass and control command flags gave the three legal frame types still used today: AD (sequence-controlled data), BD (expedited data) and BC (control commands); the fourth combination was illegal.3638 Two control commands configured the receiver: UNLOCK (one octet 0x00) and SET V(R) (0x82 0x00 plus the new value).37

    Real bytes from 1993: an UNLOCK frame from the PSS-04-151 test sequences

    Appendix B of PSS-04-151 gives encoded test CLTUs for decoder test jigs, for a mission with spacecraft ID 0x123 and virtual channel ID 0x12. Its first CLTU, a BC frame carrying UNLOCK, is printed as 55 EB 90 / 31 23 48 07 00 00 EC 0E / 95 55 55 55 55 55 55 70 / 55 55 55 55 55 55 55 55 (bytes read from the page image).61 Read with the PSS-04-107 layout: after a leading 55, 0xEB90 is the start sequence; the first codeblock carries the frame header 31 23 48 07 00, the control command 00 and the first CRC octet EC, then the parity octet 0E; the second codeblock carries the second CRC octet 95 and six fill octets 55, then its parity octet 70; the last eight 55 octets are the tail sequence. The expected frame analysis report lists two codeblocks, no corrections, an accepted BC frame, input 0 and last MAP 63.613337

    Inference

    This wiki recomputed the check fields of that test CLTU: the CRC with the PSS polynomial and all-ones preset over 31 23 48 07 00 00 gives 0xEC95, and the (63,56) BCH parity octets of the two codeblocks come out as 0x0E and 0x70, matching the printed bytes. The recomputation is this wiki's, not part of the source.

    COP-1 at the sending end (FOP-1) had six states: Active, Retransmit without Wait, Retransmit with Wait, Initialising without BC Frame, Initialising with BC Frame, and Initial. The receiving FARM-1 had three: Open, Wait and Lockout.4041 The FARM sliding window W was an even number from 2 to 254, fixed for the whole mission unless otherwise specified, with 200 the largest recommended value, and split into equal positive and negative halves (PW = NW = W/2).41 PSS-04-107 also described systematic retransmission for deep space, setting the FOP-1 timer to its minimum so that a sequence is repeated a fixed number of times without waiting for acknowledgement.46 ECSS-E-ST-50-04C later called this older procedure COP-1A and its own version COP-1B; the differences affect only the sending end.93 The CCSDS 232.1 issue of 2010 added a systematic retransmission option.125

    The 1992 CLCW has the field sizes of today's CLCW. The differences are names and two values: CCSDS now calls it the Communications (not Command) Link Control Word, bit 23 "Report Type" became a reserved spare, and the status field may be used by the mission instead of being fixed at zero.3912493

    Segments, MAPs and packets¶

    Every TC frame carried a segment: a one-octet header and up to 248 data octets (fewer when an authentication trailer was appended). The 6-bit MAP identifier gave up to 64 Multiplexer Access Points per virtual channel; PSS-04-107 could split them into 32 data MAPs and 32 control MAPs by bit 2, pairing one of each per Packet Assembly Controller (PAC). A one-octet control segment 111xxxxx reset a PAC that had entered lockout.4243

    The TC packet of 1992 already used version 0b000 (Version 1), the value every Space Packet uses today, while the telemetry packet of 1988 used 0b100.449109 Its sequence flags marked components of a higher-layer data structure rather than segments, and its optional 16-bit packet error control field could be the frame CRC or a 16-bit checksum to be defined in the PUS.44 The receiving application was to check the error field, then version, type, APID and length, and report "clean and conforming", "clean but not conforming" or "rejected, dirty" by telemetry; compare the acceptance reports of ST[01].45

    Network services and authentication¶

    ESA ground stations offered three telecommand interfaces to the control centre: Service 1, sequence-controlled delivery of packets that the station segments; Service 2, the same for packets pre-segmented and signed by the control centre; and Service 3, expedited delivery of ready-made segments of 1 to 249 octets.31 Section 10 specified optional encrypted authentication of TC segments: a plain-text segment with an appended signature, computed from a secret key, the data and a count, so that recorded commands could not be replayed. The authentication tail held a 2-bit logical authentication channel (LAC) identifier, a 30-bit LAC count and an N-octet signature; three LACs were foreseen (principal, auxiliary, recovery). COP-1 control frames were not authenticated.47 PSS-04-107 already notes that the decoder specification uses a 5-octet signature, making a 9-octet tail, and PSS-04-151 fixed the LAC identifiers: 00 principal, 01 auxiliary, 10 recovery.4756

    The standard recommended that the uplink bit error rate be guaranteed better than 10-5; at that rate it guaranteed a frame rejection probability better than 1 in 105 and an undetected error probability better than 1 in 1019.48 ECSS-E-ST-50-04C later left authentication outside its scope and dropped the segment trailer.93 How a control centre hands packets, frames or CLTUs to a station today is described under SLE, in particular the Forward Space Packet and Forward CLTU services, and link-layer authentication under security in CCSDS space links.

    PSS-04-151: the Telecommand Decoder Specification (1992, 1993)¶

    PSS-04-151 is a functional design specification of the on-board end of PSS-04-107: coding, transfer, segmentation and authentication layers, plus the Command Pulse Distribution Unit (CPDU).51 Its cover says September 1992, but the change record, page headers and copyright line say September 1993; PSS-07-101 and ECSS-E-70-41A cite it as September 1993.506698

    Requirements. The decoder was to be redundant (typically two chains), always ready without "wake-up" procedures, return to a defined "cold start" state after power loss, accept at least 4 (preferably 6) symbol stream inputs, and hold re-programmable "mission-specific data": spacecraft ID (10 bits), VC ID (6 bits), FARM window widths PW and NW (8 bits each), an authenticated-MAP pointer (5 bits), the 2940-bit fixed authentication key and the CPDU application ID (11 bits).5254 It notes the 4 kHz maximum uplink symbol rate of the RF standard, with about 20 kHz expected within the next decade, and the undetected-error target that ESA preferred (10-14) over CCSDS's (10-9).53

    Authentication unit. The signature was 40 bits. A secret 60-bit linear feedback shift register hashed the segment, the 32-bit LAC value and 24 bits of virtual fill into a 60-bit pre-signature; a "hard knapsack" of sixty secret 48-bit weights turned it into a 48-bit sum, of which 40 bits were kept. The full key was therefore 2940 bits, held twice: a fixed key in ROM for start-up and recovery and a programmable key in RAM for normal use.5556

    CPDU. The CPDU let the ground reconfigure the spacecraft when only the expedited service worked. It took one complete packet per segment (normally on MAP 0), checked an even length, a 16-bit CRC, the version (000), type (1), data field header flag (0), its own APID and sequence flags (11), then executed 2-octet instructions: one of 256 pulse outputs and a pulse length of 2k × D, where D was 10 to 15 ms and k the 3-bit code. A packet held 1 to 120 instructions (10 to 248 octets).5758

    Telemetry reports. The decoder produced a 16-bit CLCW status report (the second half of the CLCW), a 16-bit CPDU status report, an 80-bit authentication unit status report and a 32-bit Frame Analysis Report for every CLTU.5960

    Early implementations. Appendix C is a rare first-hand record. The decoder's forerunner was an ESA contract specification, THB/CS/2077/av, first issued in 1986 when two identical contracts were placed with Selenia Spazio (later Alenia Spazio) and Saab Space, and last updated in May 1988. It had a fixed, asymmetric FARM window (W = 128, PW = 120, NW = 8), no in-flight programmable hash coefficients and no minimum authenticated segment length. The appendix also says that issue 1 of PSS-04-107 was only published at the beginning of 1991, although its change record dates it June 1990. It then lists the remaining non-compliances of both companies' chips: ESA waived Alenia Spazio's coding-layer and telemetry-report deviations, while Alenia's authentication chip still awaited the upgrades; Saab Space's chip lacked the upgrades and the company intended to redesign it.626327 Appendix B adds that ESA's telecommand reference software, recommended for building decoder test equipment, lacked FOP-1's Suspend and Resume states, which it says CCSDS had defined very late for deep-space needs, as well as the authentication software.61

    Afterwards. ECSS never replaced PSS-04-151; the 2008 ECSS handbook notes that it contained important information and that ECSS was considering a decoder handbook.91 Its CPDU chapter was transcribed into Annex D of ECSS-E-70-41A, and the CPDU packet survives in ECSS-E-ST-70-41C as the one telecommand without a secondary header.98103 See ST[02] Device access.

    PSS-07-101: the first Packet Utilisation Standard (1994)¶

    Origins¶

    According to Kaufeler, ESA set up the Committee for Operations and EGSE Standards (COES) in 1987 to define what a satellite check-out system (EGSE) and a satellite control system have in common, so that one system could serve pre-launch tests and operations. Packets made many implementations of the same control function possible, so COES's first task became a standard for how packets are used. A prototype called PUSV, with a reference satellite model PUSSAT, validated it in 1992; after an Agency-level review in 1993 the Inspector General approved it.7577 PSS-07-101 sits in the PSS-07 "Ground Segment Infrastructure" branch, under the Mission Data Utilisation Standards (PSS-07-10) next to orbit and navigation, station monitoring and control, and time data utilisation standards (PSS-07-102 to -104).68

    What it defined¶

    The standard is a set of operational requirements (chapter 3), service specifications (chapters 4 to 21, summarised in chapter 22) and parameter types (chapter 23).67 It was meant as a "shopping list": missions de-select what they do not need, document the result in a mission ground/space ICD, and implement each chosen service at least at its minimum capability set.69 It did not standardise science data content, but required science packets to carry the data field header.69

    Around these headers PSS-07-101 fixed the primary header values for ESA missions: telecommand packets version 0 (value 0), sequence flags 11, and a sequence count split into a mission-sized source ID and a per-source count; telemetry packets "Version 2 (value = 4)", segmentation flags 11, a never-short-cycled count per APID, APID 0 for the time packet and all ones for idle packets. The data field header was mandatory except in CPDU commands and the time packet, and the packet error control was mandatory in telecommands.7071

    The mid-1990s papers: the PUS and XMM¶

    Kaufeler's paper summarises the standard for an outside audience: 17 services, 256 service types and 256 subtypes with 0 to 127 reserved, a data field header identical for TM and TC except for the optional time, and tailoring through capability sets, mission-optional fields and mission-chosen encoding lengths. It notes a NASA/JPL proposal for packets with several service data units as a reason for future PUS versions, and says that ESOC's SCOS-II would give full application-level support to PUS missions.7677

    Nye's paper on XMM shows "missionisation" of the still unapproved PUS. XMM was due for launch at the end of 1999, with up to 22 hours of contact a day and no bulk on-board storage. According to the paper it was to be the first ESA science spacecraft using the ESA packet standards and the first ESOC science mission on the new flight control infrastructure due in 1995.78 It grouped its packet types into on-line control, safety management and operations maintenance:79

    XMM's type numbers do not follow PSS-07-101 Table 4-1 (for example verification is 3 at XMM but 1 in the PUS, housekeeping 1 at XMM but 3 in the PUS).     The closest modern counterpart of PSS-04-107's segment authentication is CCSDS Space Data Link Security, which authenticates at the data link layer.129 No source in the library links the two. Similarly, PSS-07-101 lists a "Packet Data Description Standard" (PSS-07-201) and "Space Segment Operability Requirements" (PSS-07-501) as "to be issued";66 their subjects match what ECSS later covered in ECSS-E-ST-70-31C and ECSS-E-ST-70-11C, but the sources do not say that one grew out of the other.

    The ECSS handbook of 2008 sums up the policy shift. ESA had not adopted CCSDS books directly but republished modified versions as PSS documents; ECSS first continued that practice for the core TM/TC standards, then began to phase it out in favour of adoption notices. It also notes that the PUS was developed by ESA and ECSS independently of CCSDS, and that ECSS combined in one standard the telecommand protocols that CCSDS splits into three books.8990 How the adoption notices work is on ECSS-E-ST-50 communications.

    The parallel lineage: ESOC's control systems¶

    The standards and ESOC's mission control systems (MCS) evolved together. In 1974 ESOC decided to build reusable MCS infrastructure; the first generation, MSSS, entered service in 1977, and SCOS-I followed in 1989 for Hipparcos, ERS-1 and EURECA.80 The SCOS-II paper required the new system to be compatible with the CCSDS and ESA packet standards and with COES's standards and guidelines. Its Release 1, with a preliminary delivery expected in November 1994, added a commanding function that SCOS-I lacked; Huygens, Artemis and Envisat were the planned first clients.8081 The companion paper on the SCOS-II Operations Language recalls that MSSS derived parameters were specified on paper and coded by software engineers, and SCOS-I ones were written in FORTRAN by operations engineers.82 ESA's Bulletin later dated the start of work on SCOS to about 1992 and its first missions (Huygens, Meteosat MTP LEOP, Teamsat) to 1997.84

    What survives¶

    Reading the PSS standards explains several details of today's books:

    • The Space Packet's 7 to 65 542-octet range, the meaning of the sequence flags and the idle APID of all ones are unchanged since 1988.1112108111110
    • The TM frame header and the reserved first header pointer values are unchanged; the segment length identifier is a fossil of telemetry packet segmentation.1617116119
    • The TC frame's two spare bits after the VC ID became frame-length bits, which is why a TC frame can be 1024 octets today but only 256 in PSS-04-107.93122
    • The CLCW, the AD/BD/BC frame types, UNLOCK and SET V(R) are recognisably those of 1992.393637124123
    • PUS service numbers 1 to 6, 8, 9, 11 to 15 and 17, the 8-bit type and subtype, the 0 to 127 standard range and the PTC/PFC codes come from PSS-07-101.727073100
    • The CPDU packet still has no secondary header, as in PSS-04-151, whose CPDU required the data field header flag to be 0.57103
