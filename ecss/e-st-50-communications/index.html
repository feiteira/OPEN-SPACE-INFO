    ECSS-E-ST-50C communications and the CCSDS adoption notices¶

    ECSS-E-ST-50C ("Space engineering – Communications") is the top-level ECSS standard for the end-to-end data communication system of a spacecraft. It does not define frame formats. It defines the vocabulary, the engineering process with its documents and reviews, and the functional and performance requirements that the chosen protocols must satisfy; the protocols themselves are specified by lower-level ECSS standards, by ECSS adoption notices of CCSDS standards and by the CCSDS books.12 The current issue is Revision 2 of 5 December 2024. It changes no requirement of Revision 1 (1 March 2021) beyond correcting a typing error in requirement 5.2.5.3c: it deletes twelve definitions, fixes that typo and stops naming the issue of each adopted CCSDS book, so that the standard need not be revised every time CCSDS updates one of them.136 This page therefore follows Rev.1 and says where Rev.2 differs. The clause numbers and requirement identifiers are the same in both, but from clause 3.3 onwards each clause sits one PDF page earlier in Rev.2 (see What Rev.2 changed). It also covers all six adoption notices, ECSS-E-AS-50-21 to -26 (for TM coding, the TM, AOS and TC Space Data Link Protocols, TC coding and COP-1), the superseded 2008 standards ECSS-E-ST-50-03C (TM transfer frame) and ECSS-E-ST-50-04C (telecommand), and the communications guidelines handbook ECSS-E-HB-50A. The general ECSS context is on the ECSS overview page, and the PSS standards from which the European choices descend are on the history page.

    ECSS-E-ST-50C (Rev.1 and Rev.2)¶

    Scope¶

    The standard specifies requirements for developing the end-to-end data communication system of a spacecraft: the terminology of space communication systems engineering, the activities of the engineering process (following ECSS-E-ST-10), and specific functional and performance requirements. It covers space-to-ground, ground-to-space and space-to-space links used in operations, and the links used during assembly, integration and test (AIT).2 The communication system has three domains, the ground network, the space link and the space network; the standard covers the space link and the space network in detail and the ground network only as far as end-to-end services need it, the rest being left to ECSS-E-ST-70.2 The handbook calls ECSS-E-ST-50 a process-oriented standard, with no options, that should be applied to all missions.8

    The standard is explicit that it cannot define a complete link on its own: the complete set of standards for a link is project-dependent, and ECSS-E-HB-50 gives guidance and examples.1

    Architecture: elements, domains and the protocol stack¶

    The example configuration of the standard has a spacecraft (possibly with a probe or lander on a space-to-space link), a ground station, an operational control centre (OCC) and a mission experiment centre (MEC). Each element has a data handling system that manages its internal links, its external links and the processing between them; the downlink and uplink chains are described as acquisition, formatting, transmission, reception, de-formatting and onward delivery.11

    The three domains have very different characteristics:12

    Domain Character What the standard focuses on
    Space network (on-board and inter-spacecraft) On-board networks are varied, often proprietary, conservative and not upgradeable in flight; inter-spacecraft links are intermittent and of variable topology Upper-layer services that leave freedom of on-board data link and physical layers
    Space link Point-to-point wireless link, inherently unreliable; ground end unconstrained, space end constrained in power, mass and volume; Doppler and variable propagation; must tolerate very high BER Reliable data transfer services
    Ground network Reliable terrestrial links that can be upgraded during the mission Only the transfer of spacecraft data between ground and space nodes

    Spacecraft control considerations¶

    Clause 4.7 explains why the requirements look as they do. Under degraded conditions (on-board failures, bad attitude, tumbling) the minimum goal is to keep some control: in the worst case, commanding in the blind with some certainty of execution, which implies telecommands decoded and executed directly by hardware with minimal on-board functionality; in less extreme cases, receiving critical telemetry acquired by simple and reliable functions that usually do not rely on software. Data unit sizes are chosen to limit the effect of bit errors and drop-outs, and acknowledgements are desirable for critical command functions without preventing blind commanding.14 Control and payload traffic also differ in kind: commands must be delivered in order and may be repeated but not lost, while payload transfer aims at volume, can accept some loss and does not care about order if the data can be reconstituted.15

    What a profile looks like in practice

    The CSPD records spacecraft identifier, address and channel assignments. For Solar Orbiter the equivalent values are in the TM/TC and packet structure ICD: spacecraft identifier 650 (0x28A), a single 1115-octet frame length and a fixed allocation of data classes to virtual channels.2425

    Functional and performance requirements¶

    The remaining clauses are short "shall" and "should" statements. They are listed here in full (as paraphrases) because together they explain most protocol features of the CCSDS stack.

    5.3 Space communication system. Bandwidth is allocated according to data needs and spacecraft mode, with priority for essential commands and telemetry in emergencies; data must not be lost through congestion (buffering and flow control are suggested); all transmissions must be stoppable by telecommand at any time, which makes those telecommands essential telecommands.26

    What \(10^{-2}/N\) means (illustrative arithmetic)

    A mission that expects to uplink \(N = 10^6\) telecommands must keep the probability of accepting an erroneous one below \(10^{-2}/10^{6} = 10^{-8}\) per telecommand. The standard's note to 5.6.11.7 says that with the BCH coding of ECSS-E-AS-50-24 and the frame error control of ECSS-E-AS-50-25 the undetected frame error probability can be kept below \(10^{-18}\) at the reference BER of \(10^{-5}\), and below \(10^{-8}\) without frame error control.30 The numbers for \(N\) are illustrative.

    The Packet Assembly Controller¶

    The Packet Assembly Controller (PAC) is an ESA feature inherited from the PSS standards. CCSDS 232.0-B does not include it, and the 2008 handbook noted that because of it ECSS segments could be one octet long whereas CCSDS segments had at least two.58 ECSS-E-ST-50-04C had defined it in its segmentation sublayer (§5.5.4) and listed it among its differences from CCSDS.198205 The notice re-introduces it as an optional function used by the MAP Packet Extraction Function (for packets) and by the MAP Reception Function (for MAP_SDUs); despite its name it handles both.59

    MAP pairs. Each PAC instance uses two MAP Identifiers: a data MAP with the most significant bit '0' (identifiers 0 to 31) and a control MAP with the same five low bits and the most significant bit '1' (identifiers 32 to 63), so control MAP = data MAP + 32. Segments carrying data use the data MAP; control segments use the control MAP.6061

    A control segment is exactly one octet, has Sequence Flags '11' and the identifier of a control MAP; any valid control segment is a MAP Reset command. A Frame Data Unit on a control MAP that breaks these rules is an invalid control segment.6263

    Behaviour. On the data MAP the PAC reassembles packets or MAP_SDUs using the Sequence Flags. It keeps a reassembly status flag ('1' while a reassembly is in progress, '0' when complete) and a lockout status flag ('1' in lockout). It enters lockout on an incorrect sequence of Sequence Flags or an invalid control segment; in lockout it neither reconstructs nor delivers, and it stays there until it receives a MAP Reset. A MAP Reset discards any partially reconstructed unit, clears the reassembly flag and exits lockout.64656667

    Secondary header and the extended virtual channel frame count¶

    The secondary header is 2 to 64 octets, fixed-length per master or virtual channel in a phase, and consists of an 8-bit identification (2-bit version '00' and 6-bit length = total length minus one) followed by mission-defined data.8788 ECSS-E-ST-50-03C adds an optional standard use of it: a 4-octet secondary header (length field = 3) whose 24-bit data field counts the roll-overs of the 8-bit Virtual Channel Frame Count, giving a 32-bit count with the 24 most significant bits in the secondary header. The extension is associated with a master channel (present in every frame, carrying the count of the frame's own VC) or with one virtual channel.8990

    The handbook explains that this is ESA PSS heritage not specified by CCSDS: it helps sequence frames, resolve ambiguities and detect losses without ground receive time, especially when recorded frames are played back through a real-time virtual channel. It is optional, does not affect frame-level interoperability (SLE RAF/RCF), and excludes any other use of the secondary header on that channel.91

    Solar Orbiter uses it. Its frame ICD requires a secondary header in every frame, with identification 0b00000011 and a 3-octet data field holding an additional 24 bits of the virtual channel frame count. The same ICD fixes the frame length at 1115 octets (8920 bits), sets the Synchronization Flag to 0, the Packet Order Flag to 0, the Segment Length Identifier to '11', inserts a CLCW in the OCF of every frame and a frame error control word in every frame, and reserves VC7 for idle frames with First Header Pointer 0b11111111110.929394

    Data field, idle data and asynchronous insertion¶

    With the Synchronization Flag at 0 the data field carries packets whose Packet Version Number is authorised by CCSDS 135.0-B-3. Packets are placed contiguously per virtual channel, may span frames, may have different version numbers on one VC, and idle packets (an idle Space Packet, at least 7 octets, or a fill Encapsulation Packet) or frames of only idle data fill gaps. The receiver extracts packets with the First Header Pointer and the packet length fields and discards idle data and idle packets.959697

    For legacy systems that recorded whole TM frames (for example on a tape recorder), clause 5.4.4 lets recorded frames be played back inside the data field of real-time frames with the Synchronization Flag set to 1, on a dedicated virtual channel, in forward or reverse mode. In forward mode the recorded sync markers must use the alternative pattern 0x352EF853 so that they do not disturb frame synchronization; recorded CLCWs must not be processed by the real-time telecommand system.98 The handbook recommends using this only for legacy support, and notes that AOS frames handle playback with their Replay Flag instead.99100

    Operational Control Field and Frame Error Control Field¶

    The OCF (4 octets, after the data field) is associated with a master or virtual channel and present in every frame of it in a phase. Bit 0 is the Type Flag: '0' means a Type-1-Report, a CLCW per ECSS-E-ST-50-04 clause 6.3; '1' means a Type-2-Report, whose bit 1 says project-specific ('0') or reserved ('1').101102 The handbook recommends using the OCF only for CLCWs.99

    The 16-bit FECF follows the OCF (or the data field). It is mandatory unless the frame is Reed-Solomon encoded, present in every frame of the physical channel in a phase, and is computed as \(\text{FECF} = [X^{16} M(X) + X^{n-16} L(X)] \bmod G(X)\) with \(G(X) = X^{16}+X^{12}+X^5+1\) and the register preset to all ones; the receiver computes a syndrome that is zero for an error-free frame. It detects all odd numbers of bit errors, all patterns of up to two errors and all single bursts of up to 16 bits in blocks under 32 768 bits, and misses a random even-weight (at least 4) error pattern with probability of about \(2^{-15}\). It is never used for correction.103104105

    Recommended practice¶

    Telecommand.118119

    • Use the sequence-controlled service for MAPs that use segmentation, because Sequence Flags have no counter and reassembly needs complete, ordered delivery.
    • Assign TC virtual channel identifiers with maximum Hamming distance between them, so that a bit error in an on-board CLCW does not turn one VC into another, and avoid identifiers of all zeros or all ones, which a failed or unpowered decoder can produce.
    • The No RF Available and No Bit Lock flags report the physical channel as a whole, not a single decoder; the CLCW VC field carries the full 6-bit identifier.
    • Near-Earth missions should use the COP-1 sequence-controlled (AD) service and keep the expedited (BD) service for exceptional cases such as recovery; COP-1 retransmission is less efficient for deep space, where an additional mechanism was being studied in 2008.
    • Prefer PLOP-2 to PLOP-1 for its higher throughput.
    • There is no ECSS telecommand decoder specification; the withdrawn ESA PSS-04-151 contained important information, and the PUS has an annex on the CPDU and its interaction with PSS decoders. The PSS specification, issue 1 of September 1992, is now in the library as ESA PSS-04-151.207

    The transponder "dual lock" issue. Near-Earth and Lagrange-point spacecraft such as Cryosat-2 combine the uplink from both low-gain antennas with a 3 dB hybrid so that both transponders see the best signal. Without such care, one receiver can run near its threshold (the handbook quotes about −122 dBm) with a poor bit error rate while the other is well above it; the decoder may then select the poor channel and reject telecommands. The handbook recommends the AD service for all near-Earth missions, a realistic pre-launch compatibility test with representative ground station and control system, and mission-specific tuning of COP-1 parameters to the Earth–spacecraft distance.120121 ECSS-E-ST-50C Rev.1 turned the lesson into requirement 5.4.9a.29

    Telemetry.99122

    • Use the OCF only for the CLCW; use asynchronously inserted data only for legacy support.
    • Use virtual channels to separate sources with different characteristics (for example an imager with very long packets on its own VC) or different destinations.
    • Choose the maximum packet length with latency in mind: while a long packet is sent, other sources on the VC wait.
    • Do not feed frames with uncorrectable errors to packet extraction, and signal a gap whenever a frame is missing or erroneous, detected with the master, virtual and extended counts.

    Channel coding. For telecommand ECSS-E-ST-50-04 left no coding options. For telemetry the handbook tabulates coding gain against bandwidth expansion (frame length 8920 bits, AWGN, BPSK), from Reed-Solomon (255,223) alone (bandwidth ×1.14, about 5.4 dB gain at FER \(10^{-4}\)) through concatenated Reed-Solomon and convolutional codes to turbo rate ¼ (×4, about 11.7 dB). It advises trading coding gain against bandwidth limits, link budget and decoder complexity, noting for example that turbo rate ½ gains about 5 dB over convolutional rate ½ at the same bandwidth but was hard to decode in real time above about 20 Mbit/s.123124 Details are on the TM coding page.

    Idle data. TM and AOS channels are synchronous and must fill every frame slot; TC and Proximity-1 are asynchronous and need no idle data in frames (PLOP-2 sends an idle sequence between CLTUs instead). Idle patterns are project-specific; all-zero or periodic patterns can upset bit synchronization, so projects without randomization must choose carefully.125126
