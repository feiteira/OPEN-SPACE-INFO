    ECSS-E-ST-70C: ground systems and operations¶

    ECSS-E-ST-70C ("Space engineering – Ground systems and operations", 31 July 2008) is the top-level ECSS standard of the E-70 discipline. It holds the basic rules, principles and requirements for engineering the ground segment and for preparing and conducting mission operations, and it defines the customer-supplier relationships for both: a customer with a ground segment supplier (GSS) and a customer with an operations supplier (OS).1 It does not cover EGSE and the operations that support space segment verification (that is ECSS-E-ST-10-02) nor the launch segment and its operations.1

    Where the other E-70 standards on this wiki describe things - the spacecraft's operability (ECSS-E-ST-70-11), its monitoring and control data (ECSS-E-ST-70-31), procedure languages on the ground (ECSS-E-ST-70-32) and on board (ECSS-E-ST-70-01), and the packet services (ECSS-E-ST-70-41, the PUS) - this standard describes the processes and documents in which those things are produced, validated and used, and it lists the others as its level 3 standards.66

    What changed from the A issues¶

    The 2008 issue replaced the phase-based approach of ECSS-E-70A with a process approach: operations engineering and ground segment engineering are separate processes (and disciplines), projected onto the ECSS-M-10 lifecycle model. It identifies separate customer-supplier relationships for operations and for the ground segment and accepts different procurement models (one organisation supplying both, or two). It aligns with ECSS-E-ST-10C system engineering in processes, documents, reviews and terminology, reviews the interfaces with spacecraft design and manufacture (adding the CFISRD), assumes no reference architecture for the ground segment (functions are listed without saying how they are grouped), and brings the document requirements definitions (DRDs) into normative annexes. New DRDs are the mission analysis report (MAR), mission operations plan (MOP), customer furnished items and services requirements document (CFISRD), operations engineering plan (OEP) and ground segment system engineering plan.2

    The domain (clause 4)¶

    The domain of the standard has two components: the ground segment (every ground system used to prepare, conduct and close operations) and the operations organisations (the people). An entity combines an operations organisation with its ground systems, for example a control centre with its mission control team, or a ground station with its operations and maintenance staff.7 Teams typically include operations managers, spacecraft operators (astronauts, operations engineers, spacecraft analysts, spacecraft controllers), payload operators, mission planners, flight dynamics engineers, ground systems operators, mission exploitation staff and ground systems maintenance engineers.8

    Two engineering disciplines act on this domain: operations engineering (preparing and executing mission operations) and ground segment engineering (defining, designing, producing, integrating, verifying and maintaining the ground segment). They are separate process chains with many links, and both contribute to the space segment requirements and to reviews of the on-board design.10 The standard singles out critical areas for coordination: mission concepts with due weight on ground and operations aspects; spacecraft operability and maintainability; the adequacy of what the operations customer delivers; end-to-end validation; reuse of operations knowledge and data (TM/TC definitions, procedures) from spacecraft design through AIT to operations; commonality between ground services and between AIT and operations; security and safety; and communications design and cost.11

    5.2 Requirements analysis and concept development¶

    The inputs are the operations customer requirements document (OCRD, the "initial technical specification" of ECSS-E-ST-10-06 tailored to operations, written with the end users), the mission description document (mission analysis, system description and operations scenarios for each concept), the space-system trade-off report and the launcher user manual; the first three are phase 0 products.14

    • Mission analysis characterises launcher, ground station, spacecraft, mission-specific (for example station-keeping windows, orbit phasing), payload and data flow constraints, and supplies 13 items: launch window, separation sequence, target orbit, positioning strategy, orbit determination concept, schedule of operational events (nominal and planned contingencies), station coverage, analysis of high-risk sequences, interference risk with flying spacecraft, delta-V budget, environmental impacts (sensor blinding, eclipses), payload operations strategy and data circulation scheme. Output: the MAR.15
    • Operational analysis checks the customer requirements, assesses feasibility, finds the drivers, defines the mission operations concept and derives requirements on operations engineering and the ground segment, and contributes to interface definition. Output: the mission operations concept document (MOCD).16
    • Operational interfaces: space segment operability requirements are compiled in the space segment operability requirements document (SSORD), described as a tailored version of ECSS-E-ST-70-11; on-board mission operations services and their requests and reports are defined with the OC (ECSS-E-ST-70-41 can be tailored for this, or a standalone document or the space-to-ground ICD used); external interfaces go into operational ICDs (contacts, services, data exchange formats and methods) that later join the MOP; and the deliverables and services required from the OC go into the CFISRD.17
    • The operations engineering plan (OEP) gives the schedule for producing and validating mission operations data, the team organisation and the recruitment profile; it exists so that operations manpower can be costed and recruited on time, and may be merged with the GSEP when GSS and OS are one organisation.18
    • User requirements are written for the ground segment and each ground system and for validation, training and simulation tools, covering functionality and performance, automation requirements matching the operations concept and staffing profile, centralisation or decentralisation, user management and human-computer interaction.19
    • The operational validation plan (OVP) covers test preparation, resources, strategies, methods, scenarios, pass/fail criteria and post-test activities.20

    The CFISRD items listed in §5.2.2.3f are worth reading in full because they are exactly the interfaces between spacecraft project and operations: space segment documents (SSUM, detailed design, spacecraft monitoring and control database); procedures validated during spacecraft AIT; representative TM/TC samples including payload data; a suitcase for TT&C compatibility tests; access to the spacecraft on ground; the operational simulator and space segment models; on-board software and documentation; data processing tools (mission-specific decommutation, derived-parameter algorithms); the software development environment and validation benches for on-board software maintenance; engineering support during ground segment design and phases E and F; support for in-orbit control in designated phases; and site infrastructure.17

    5.3 Mission operations data production¶

    The inputs are the MAR, MOCD and operational ICDs from the OS; the space segment user manual (SSUM), detailed design documents and space segment monitoring and control database from the OC; and the ground systems user manuals and ground segment monitoring and control database from the GSS.22 The process documents everything needed for in-orbit monitoring and control in the mission operations plan (MOP): organisation, responsibilities, decision making and major mission rules; the sequence of main events of all critical phases (station visibility, major operations); timed operations schedules for every critical operation (each team member's activities, interfaces, external constraints; LEOP, commissioning, fly-bys, orbit insertions); operations procedures for nominal and contingency operations of both segments; and disposal procedures.23 Further rules:23

    • An independently operated entity gets a sub-plan of the MOP (should).
    • Every operations procedure states its required authorisation level, applicability, and execution conditions and constraints before, during and after execution.
    • Space segment procedures are made by expanding the procedures in the SSUM with ground segment specifics.
    • The monitoring and control databases are populated with mission data for space and ground segment processing, which includes mission planning data, OBCPs, TM/TC definitions, display definitions, on-board software images and patches.
    • Mission exploitation data (processing databases, user databases) and a flight dynamics schedule are established.
    • Before delivery, the OC verifies its inputs against representative space segment models, at least the space segment monitoring and control database and the SSUM procedures.

    The outputs are the MOP and the space and ground segment monitoring and control databases.24

    5.4 Mission operations data validation¶

    Mission operations data must be validated to show that it is correct and compatible with the space segment; inspection is enough for some data (parameter descriptions, non-testable procedures). Validation uses tools representative of the space segment design - chiefly the operational simulator - and a flight-representative space segment, exercising all operations procedures and telecommands, all spacecraft modes including non-nominal ones, and all redundancies (within the limits of operating space hardware on the ground). These tests are sometimes called system validation tests (SVTs). Results and the validation status of each data item are recorded.25

    5.5 Operations teams build-up and training¶

    Operations are carried out by teams; a typical organisation has a mission control team, a flight dynamics team (orbit and attitude determination and prediction, manoeuvre preparation, sensor calibration), ground operations teams (stations, communications, control centre), a mission exploitation team (payload data planning, processing and distribution) and ground segment and space segment support teams of experts. Roles are defined per team member and mission phase; teams are trained before operational validation starts through theory, realistic simulations, rehearsals of operational and contingency scenarios; training is documented in the operations training plan (OTP) and recorded per person.26

    5.6 Operational validation¶

    Operational validation takes place in a realistic operational context and demonstrates that the whole ground segment and its interfaces work, that all mission operations data is correct, that all staff are qualified and that the teams can support the mission (for LEOP, the LEOP network and its staff too). It includes:27

    Activity Purpose
    Simulations and rehearsals Teams execute nominal and contingency scenarios of the MOP with the operational simulator in place of the spacecraft
    Mission readiness tests (MRTs) Validate ground station readiness and train station and network staff
    Data flow tests (DFTs) Validate TM, TC and tracking interfaces between stations and control centre, and between other ground systems (for example payload data circulation)
    Tracking campaigns Validate end-to-end ranging and Doppler with missions already flying

    It follows the OVP, records unexpected behaviour in the operational validation report, ends with a ground segment acceptance report, is supported by the GSS, and wraps major tests in test readiness and post-test reviews per ECSS-E-ST-10-02. Afterwards, change authority for the ground segment - including shared infrastructure such as LANs, firewalls, power, cabling and buildings - passes to a formal change control board.27

    5.7 Operational configuration management¶

    Mission operations data and the space segment are kept functional and under configuration control (per ECSS-M-ST-40) through preparation and execution; changes go through the same processes as the original design, development and verification, with definition, implementation, verification, operational validation and formal authorisation. Teams are kept operational; replacement staff are formally trained and exercised in simulations before working autonomously.28

    5.8 Operations execution¶

    Operations run from launch to disposal and divide into critical phases (LEOP, commissioning, planetary fly-by or orbit insertion, rendezvous and docking, retrieval) and routine phases (exploitation, cruise, hibernation).29

    • Critical operations are executed per the MOP by staff able to detect and respond to unexpected behaviour in line with the FDIR approach of the SSUM, with managers present for authorisations and facilities augmented as needed.30
    • Routine operations: mission planning from user requests and constraints; detailed schedules produced and executed; each activity's execution verified; health monitoring of both segments; monitoring and control data archived for troubleshooting and performance analysis; performance evaluated; payload data processed, archived and distributed with ancillary data; corrective actions or contingency procedures invoked on anomalies; a new plan and procedures produced, validated and approved for contingencies not covered by the MOP; a revised mission plan agreed with the OC after a major anomaly; and an anomaly review board (also acting as nonconformance review board per ECSS-Q-ST-10-09).31
    • Reporting: periodic operations status reports (for example daily in LEOP, weekly in routine operations), an engineering assessment report after LEOP or critical operations, a commissioning report, performance reports with trend analysis, operations anomaly reports (OARs) tracked to closure for the whole mission, and reports on operations experience, lessons learned, in-orbit performance and end-of-life tests.32

    Outputs include the spacecraft in its final orbit configured for exploitation, LEOP, commissioning and routine reports, the mission history archive (telemetry, telecommand history), plans and schedules, performance and trend data, mission products, OARs and mission reports.33

    5.9 Space segment disposal operations¶

    Disposal operations comprise analyses, development and validation of disposal products and processes, and final de-orbiting, re-entry or retrieval, with disposal or recycling of retrieved elements. The analysis covers storage, dismantling, reuse, recycling or destruction, cost and organisation; rules, procedures and timelines go into a disposal operations plan (part of the MOP) that respects space debris mitigation and launch authority requirements.34

    Ground segment engineering (clause 6)¶

    The five ground segment engineering processes are definition, production, AIT and verification, maintenance and disposal; logistics support activities are included throughout.35

    6.2 Ground segment definition¶

    The inputs are the ground segment customer requirements document (GSCRD), the MDD, the MOCD and a draft space-to-ground ICD (SGICD).36 Four sub-processes follow:37

    • Requirements engineering produces the ground segment requirements document (GSRD) from the GSCRD and the operations supplier's user requirements, covering 16 topics: functionality; performance and dependability derived from space system requirements; security; mission safety; operations and logistics support; internal and external interfaces; reuse of generic infrastructure; maintainability; location and site diversity; failure-case operations, recovery and fall-back; system management (user administration, access, equipment configuration, performance monitoring); configuration management; network design and management; commonality; interoperability (for example other agencies' stations).
    • Design engineering records the architecture, down to individual ground systems and including integrated logistics support, in the ground segment design definition file (GSDDF); defines the verification and validation approach; analyses reuse of existing and multi-mission systems; defines internal and external interfaces; identifies critical elements with their reliability, availability, maintainability and safety requirements; justifies the design in the ground segment design justification file (GSDJF); writes the ground segment engineering plan (GSEP) per the ECSS-E-ST-10 system engineering plan; produces the ground segment AIT and verification plans; and contributes RF, voice, video and high-rate data interfaces to the SGICD (pointing to CCSDS 701.0-B-2).
    • Ground systems requirements engineering writes a requirements document for each ground system, with at least functional, performance, interface, design, operational, environment, resource, RAMS, security and verification requirements, applying ECSS-E-ST-40 for software, tracing to the GSRD and generating ICDs for every external interface.
    • Logistics support preparation produces a logistics support plan covering staffing and training, spares, support equipment, software support, packaging, handling, storage and transport, and shared versus mission-procured resources.

    6.3 Ground segment production¶

    Each ground system follows the ECSS-E-ST-10 system engineering process recursively (definition, production, AIT and verification, maintenance), whether it operates the mission or supports testing, maintenance or training; an operational simulator supplied by the space segment supplier or a third party is treated as a ground system. Reuse of existing systems is analysed; ECSS-E-ST-40 and ECSS-Q-ST-80 apply to software; suppliers support delivery, installation and on-site acceptance; qualification or acceptance tests (possibly including a factory acceptance test) run against the system requirements in the target environment; acceptance reports, user manuals and maintenance manuals and procedures are produced for every system.38

    AIT uses a preliminary set of mission operations data and proceeds incrementally from low-level subsystems, with a verification test (conformance with design and interfaces) and a validation test (preliminary fitness for use) at each stage. Verification then addresses the ground segment as a whole: conformance to requirements, functional coherence and compatibility of the ground systems, compatibility with the space segment, compatibility with external entities through end-to-end tests, and a first exposure to realistic operational conditions. Any later modification requires non-regression testing.3940

    Space segment compatibility testing may use a suitcase representative of the spacecraft TT&C hardware and software, an operational simulator (with emulators or even real spacecraft components in a hybrid simulator), representative TM samples and access to the spacecraft during its AIT and at the launch site. At least two test periods are needed, long enough for the spacecraft's complexity and far enough apart to correct ground problems found in the first; listen-in tests, open-loop routing of AIT telemetry to the control centre, are usually added when possible. Compatibility tests include the mission operations data (databases, procedures, command schedules). Results are reviewed at the ground segment qualification review (GSQR).41

    6.5 Maintenance and 6.6 disposal¶

    Maintenance keeps the ground segment within its availability and performance requirements and covers obsolescence and migrations (for example when computers or operating systems reach end of service): regular performance monitoring and reports, routine maintenance per procedures with a log, incident management (log incidents and restore service), problem management (diagnose root causes, trend analysis, raise change requests), change management (impact assessment, planning, new releases into operations), configuration management (a configuration database covering hardware, network, software including COTS, documentation, configuration data and mission operations data) and obsolescence management.42 Disposal analyses storage, dismantling, reuse, recycling and destruction, keeps mission data accessible for an agreed duration, and produces a disposal report and data archives with post-mission consolidated data and access tools.43

    Lifecycle and reviews (clause 7)¶

    The ground segment processes are allocated to ECSS-M-ST-10 phases ending in formal reviews; the ground segment phases are not necessarily aligned with those of the space system, but they are strongly interdependent.     Annex I: what an operations procedure must contain¶

    An operations procedure is the elementary building block of the MOP that achieves one operational objective; it may be called by a mission timeline, an automatically executing schedule or another procedure, or started manually. Each has a unique identification number, and the DRD applies to any human-readable form, on paper or on a display:62

    Element Content
    Preliminary elements Title, version and applicability date, author(s)
    Introduction (informative, not executable) Objective, execution context, goal and all possible results, figures
    Required operational authority Special authorisation for manual execution (for example the written authority of the spacecraft operations manager)
    Expected duration How long execution takes
    Pre-conditions Conditions that must hold before start, and conditions that prohibit start; referring to mission phase, ground and space segment configuration, orbit position or environment, and the status of other procedures
    Procedure body Steps executed in sequence or in parallel; each step structured like a procedure (pre-conditions, executable body, post-conditions) with its sequence of activities: uplink a TC, initiate a command stack or predefined sequence, execute a ground command (for example select a display), check telemetry values, wait until a time, for a delta time or while a condition holds, mission-specific activities
    Contingency element What to do when the system cannot be left as it is (for example a disabled protection, or an aborted orbital manoeuvre): suspend the body, act, then resume; or reach the goal another way and stop; or abort
    Post conditions How to verify that the goals were met; final status "terminated, success confirmed" or "terminated, failed (with reasons)"

    The same structure, made executable, is the PLUTO procedure of ECSS-E-ST-70-32C, whose watchdog actions "resume", "terminate" and "abort" mirror the three contingency outcomes.6275

    FCPs, CRPs and the FOP in ESA practice

    The standard speaks of the MOP and of nominal and contingency operations procedures. ESA documents use other names for the same products. ECSS-E-ST-70-32C calls nominal and contingency procedures flight control procedures (FCPs) and places them in a "flight operations plan (FOP)".76 The Herschel/Planck OIRD names flight control procedures FCP_<subsystem>_<nnnn> and contingency recovery procedures CRP_<subsystem>_<nnnn>.79 ESOC's preparation environment plans a "FOP Editor" for flight operation procedures maintained by the flight control team, re-engineered from MOIS Writer.80 ESOC's mission plans themselves carried the title "Flight Operations Plan", as the INTEGRAL example below shows.83 In the Solar Orbiter MIB, 2 243 of the 3 404 command sequences name an FCP document as their source and 1 091 a CRP document.8281

    Commonality (Annex K)¶

    Ground segments are frequently built from existing and multi-mission systems, so the design must consider reuse with customisation and extension. Reuse happens between flight and ground segments (space link elements, mission operations data), between projects (software with the same role, such as monitoring and control, flight dynamics or planning), and in shared middleware and infrastructure. Software commonality should follow the ECSS-Q-ST-80 rules on reuse; the candidate systems are the mission operations system, the payload operations and data system, the EGSE, station monitoring and control and data functions, and simulation, test and training tools; the reusable functions include monitoring, commanding, database management, procedure preparation, management and execution, archiving and distribution, performance evaluation, on-board software maintenance and HCI.64 The EGSE and the ground stations both face the space link and should consider common downlink reception, uplink radiation and downlink and uplink data processing. Mission operations data (monitoring and control definitions, displays) should be reused between manufacturer, checkout and operations, and the monitoring and control systems used in those areas should have compatible databases.65 SCOS-2000 as EGSE and the MIB are ESA's realisation of this recommendation, and ECSS-E-ST-70-31C is the standard for the data that makes it possible.
