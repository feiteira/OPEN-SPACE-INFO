    PUS: the Packet Utilization Standard¶

    The Packet Utilization Standard (PUS), ECSS-E-ST-70-41C "Telemetry and telecommand packet utilization", defines the application-level interface between a spacecraft and its ground segment. The CCSDS Space Packet Protocol and the ECSS-E-ST-50 communication standards move packets end to end; PUS says what those packets mean: which requests the ground can send, which reports the spacecraft returns, and how both are laid out bit by bit, so that integration, testing and flight operations can rely on a common interface.1 This wiki writes PUS message types as TC[x,y] and TM[x,y], for example TM[3,25] "housekeeping parameter report" or TC[11,4] "insert activities into the time-based schedule".44 The standard did not start in the ECSS system: it began as an ESA standard, ESA PSS-07-101 of May 1994, and many of today's service and subtype numbers were fixed then (see History).7689

    At a glance

    Item Value
    Origin ESA PSS-07-101 Packet Utilisation Standard, Issue 1, May 1994, prepared by COES; PUS version number 07685
    Current issue ECSS-E-ST-70-41C, 15 April 2016 (656 pages)2
    Previous issue ECSS-E-70-41A, 30 January 2003; issue B was never published2
    Draft revision ECSS-E-ST-70-41C Rev.1 DIR1, 11 December 2024, public review until 28 February 202552
    Scope Use of TC and TM packets for remote monitoring and control of subsystems and payloads, during integration, testing and operations3
    Out of scope Mission-specific payload data packets, audio and video, on-board message transport, ground processing35
    Standard service types 20 (ST[01] to ST[23], with 7, 10 and 16 reserved)7
    Packet version on the wire PUS version number 2 in every TC and TM secondary header8

    Where PUS sits¶

    PUS complements the space packet and data link standards: it does not move bits, it defines the messages carried by them.1 Requests initiated on the ground travel as telecommand packets to an on-board application process, and reports travel back as telemetry packets; how on-board processes exchange messages among themselves is mission-dependent and outside the standard.5

    flowchart LR
  subgraph G["Ground segment"]
    U["Ground application process<br/>(subservice user)<br/>e.g. mission control system"]
  end
  subgraph L["Space link"]
    TC["TC packet<br/>request TC[x,y]"]
    TM["TM packet<br/>report TM[x,y]"]
  end
  subgraph S["Space segment"]
    P["On-board application process (APID)<br/>(subservice provider)<br/>services ST[01] ... ST[23]"]
  end
  U --> TC --> P
  P --> TM --> U

    The packets ride in TC transfer frames and TM transfer frames (or AOS frames); on the telemetry side virtual channels separate data streams, and on the telecommand side MAP identifiers create sub-channels inside a virtual channel - up to 64 per virtual channel.6 For the overall chain see downlink and uplink.

    History¶

    The PUS began as an ESA standard in 1994 and moved into the ECSS system in 2003. The wider story of the ESA packet standards and how they map onto today's CCSDS and ECSS documents is told in From ESA PSS to CCSDS and ECSS; this section follows the PUS itself.

    Before ECSS: the ESA Packet Utilisation Standard (1987-1994)¶

    Satellites used to be monitored and controlled largely at the "hardware" level: telemetry parameters sat at fixed positions in a telemetry format, and commands were fixed-length frames that loaded on-board registers or set switches. Through the 1980s more and more functions moved into on-board software, which still had to be operated with these hardware-oriented techniques - a constraint on its flexibility and on the trend towards on-board autonomy.90 The CCSDS packet recommendations, and the ESA Packet Telemetry Standard (PSS-04-106, January 1988) and Packet Telecommand Standard (PSS-04-107, April 1992) derived from them, opened a flexible end-to-end exchange between ground applications and on-board application processes, each identified by an APID - but they left the internal structure and content of the packets open.9077 (The packet layers themselves are described on Space Packet Protocol.)

    In 1987 ESA set up the Committee for Operations and EGSE Standards (COES) to define the functions common to a satellite checkout system (EGSE) and a satellite control system, so that one monitoring and control system could serve pre-launch checkout and post-launch operations, within a project and across projects. Because packets let the same control function be implemented in many different ways, COES found its task feasible only with a clear, packet-based satellite-ground interface, so its first job became a standard defining precisely how telemetry and telecommand packets are used.91

    The result was ESA PSS-07-101, Packet Utilisation Standard, Issue 1 (May 1994), prepared by COES and approved by the ESA Inspector General after an Agency-level review during 1993.7695 It defines the application-level interface between ground and space for electrical integration and testing and for flight operations, and it sits in the PSS-07 ground segment documentation tree under the "Mission Data Utilisation Standards" (PSS-07-10), next to standards for orbit and navigation data, station monitoring and control data, and time data (PSS-07-102 to PSS-07-104).77 Its scope already matches today's: monitoring and remote control of subsystems and payloads through packets, but neither the protocols between on-board application processes nor the content of science packets, and no audio or video.78

    The 1994 document has two layers. Chapter 3 states operational requirements for nominal and contingency operations, each with a mnemonic and a serial number (for example TC-1: a telecommand packet carries one and only one telecommand function), and writes mission-dependent quantities as "Mission Parameters" in angle brackets, such as <TIME_CORREL_ACCUR>, collected in Appendix B. Chapters 4 to 21 then specify the services that satisfy those requirements, with their telecommand and telemetry packets; chapter 22 summarises every request and report, and chapter 23 defines the parameter types and structure rules.798182 Every packet except CPDU commands and the spacecraft time packet has a data field header carrying a service type and a service subtype, with values 0-127 reserved for the standard and 128-255 for missions, and PUS version number 0.1358592 Seventeen standard services were defined; three of them - task management (7), time reporting (10) and on-board traffic management (16) - were not kept as separate services in the ECSS issues (PUS-A moved the time report into service 9), and PUS-C still reserves their numbers because earlier versions used them.8649757 The 1994 packet headers and the service-by-service lineage are on PUS-A vs PUS-C.

    Before approval the standard was validated by a prototyping exercise completed in 1992: a prototype called PUSV, running on one or two SPARC workstations, emulated the on-board behaviour in response to a ground control system and could model different on-board application architectures, while a reference satellite model called PUSSAT served for validation and demonstration.94 The 1994 paper that presented the standard to the community expected it to evolve incrementally as new services matured, and announced that ESOC's distributed mission control infrastructure, SCOS-II, would give full application-level support to PUS missions while COES specified a generic checkout and operations system.95 That ground-system lineage continues on SCOS-2000.

    The X-ray observatory XMM gives an early example of tailoring. A 1994 ESOC paper, written while the PUS was still awaiting approval, describes how the project selected services from the draft standard to fit a classical operations concept - real-time control from ESOC through a single ground station with up to 22 hours of contact a day and no bulk on-board storage - and calls this selection and tailoring "missionisation".9697 XMM grouped its services into on-line control, safety management and operations maintenance and numbered them its own way: periodic housekeeping was TM type 1, device commands TC type 2, telecommand verification TM type 3, time-tagged commands type 7 and on-board monitoring of up to 30 parameters type 8. Its requirements came from an XMM Operations Interface Requirements Document (OIRD) and its packets from an XMM Packet Structure Definition.9899

    From PUS-A to PUS-C and Rev.1¶

    ECSS-E-70-41A of 30 January 2003 became the first ECSS issue and introduced PUS version number 1; its footnotes record that version 0 "was used by the ESA PUS". ECSS-E-70-41B was never issued.2874 PUS-C still lists PSS-07-101 in its bibliography.9

    By January 2011 the working group preparing the third version had 190 change requests asking to remove PUS-A deficiencies, inject lessons learned, meet the needs of future missions, stay consistent with newer ECSS and CCSDS standards, follow the ECSS drafting rules and keep backward compatibility where possible.5654 The working group was convened by ESA with members from Astrium, CNES, DLR, EUMETSAT, Spacebel and TAS.55 The four-month public review of the draft (ECSS-E-ST-70-41C DIR1) ended in December 2014 with 620 discrepancy reports,56 464 of them from agencies and 156 from industry; the working group added 44 of its own in 2015, giving 664 in total, 72 of them major. Of these, 72 concerned the new PUS foundation model alone.59 The reviewers asked, among other things, for more background information, a clearer explanation of how services relate to application processes and packets, a simpler ground segment model, and more tailoring flexibility.60

    ECSS-E-ST-70-41C was published on 15 April 2016. Its change log names the main changes: the introduction of the PUS foundation model, used to produce the standard service types and to be used by missions for their own service types and services, and a proper separation of system requirements from interface requirements.2 At the ADCSS 2015 workshop the convenor summarised the result as a new, formally defined vocabulary, a system specification focused on what is expected on board, and a drafting process supported by the foundation model - about 150 generic requirements in clause 5, 2 300 system requirements in clause 6 and 750 interface requirements in clauses 7 and 8.5758

    The latest step is ECSS-E-ST-70-41C Rev.1 DIR1 (11 December 2024), a draft distributed for public review from 12 December 2024 to 28 February 2025 that "may not be referred to as an ECSS Standard until published as such".52 It deletes ST[08] and adds ST[24] to ST[27] (see PUS-A vs PUS-C).53

    The vocabulary¶

    PUS-C defines a precise set of terms; the most important are:

    Term Meaning in PUS-C
    Application process Element of the space system that hosts one or more subservice entities; on board or on ground; implemented in software, firmware or hardware.1110
    Service Functional element providing closely related, remotely operable functions; composed of subservices.12
    Subservice Elementary part of a service: exactly one subservice provider and its subservice users, interacting through dedicated messages.12
    Subservice provider / user The provider executes requests and generates reports; the user initiates requests and processes reports.12
    Capability A functionality of a service or subservice, specified as operational requirements.13
    Message A request or a report.14
    Request / instruction A request is a message of one or more instructions issued by a subservice user for execution by a provider.14
    Report / notification A report is a message of one or more notifications; three kinds: verification reports, data reports and event reports.14
    Transaction The set of messages related to one capability: request-related, autonomous data reporting, or event reporting.15

    Usually the on-board processes host the providers and the ground hosts the users, but the standard allows the reverse: ground equipment can host providers so that a control centre can command it, and a payload can host users, for example to manage a mass memory with file management requests.10

    The PUS model¶

    PUS-C is organised as a multi-layer model:16

    flowchart TB
  F["PUS foundation model<br/>(clause 5): generic concepts and rules"]
  ST["Standard service type model<br/>(clauses 6-8): ST[01] ... ST[23]"]
  MS["Mission-specific service type model<br/>tailored standard types + new types (ID 128-255)"]
  SS["Space system service model<br/>services instantiated in each application process"]
  F --> ST
  F --> MS
  ST --> MS
  MS --> SS
    • The foundation model fixes the generic object types - service type, subservice type, capability type, message, request and report types, transaction types - and the rules that every service type, standard or mission-specific, must obey.1721
    • The standard service type model is the set of service types specified by the standard. Each one has a system specification in clause 6 (semantics: what the service does and how it reacts to requests) and an interface specification in clause 8 (syntax: packet layouts, using the field types of clause 7.3).18
    • The mission-specific service type model is recorded by each mission in a "mission-specific packet utilization definition document" that lists the standard service types it uses, each tailored, plus any mission-specific service types.19
    • The space system service model deploys these types: it says which application process hosts which service instance, with its full tailored specification.20

    The 2015 presentation explains why: the foundation model was written with a formal "fact based modelling" technique to remove PUS-A ambiguities, keep all requirements consistent, ease tailoring, and enable the automatic generation of software and documentation.62 A prototype "PUS foundation database" was used to verify the consistency of the message type requirements and to generate parts of clauses 6 and 8 and the message type summary annex.63

    Tooling built on the foundation model¶

    A study called PUSCGen ("PUS C Gen"; final presentation dated 15 May 2020, slides carrying a Spacebel copyright notice; it started from an ORM model supplied by ESA and reported its findings to ESA) took this idea to a toolset. Its motivation was the size of PUS-C: it wanted to check the standard's internal consistency by producing its content systematically from the foundation model, and to make tailoring easier.120121 The study captured clause 5 in Object Role Modelling (ORM) with the NORMA tool, reviewing and extending the ESA-supplied model, and treated the specification in clauses 6 and 8 as a population of that model. One stated limitation is that the "system objects" a subservice defines or accesses (functions, memories ...) are modelled only generically.122123 The review produced 21 change requests against PUS-C - 7 major (a functional problem), 7 minor (missing internal consistency or not covered by the foundation) and 7 editorial - and a relational database generated from the ORM model.124

    On that database the study built three cooperating tools:125126127128

    Tool What it does
    Population Tool defines service, subservice, capability, message, instruction and notification types, and their arguments; tailors them per mission, subject to the dependency and exclusion constraints of the population
    DOC-GEN regenerates clauses 6 and 8 and Annexes C and D in ECSS-E-ST-70-41C form; a population of the whole standard recreates it "with good fidelity", and in many cases a generated page is "hard to distinguish" from the original
    ASN1-GEN turns a tailored population into ASN.1/ACN definitions of the TC and TM, which ESA's ASN1SCC compiler converts into data structures, C encoders and decoders (with optional Python bindings) and ICDs

    The study observed that almost all PUS-C services follow a very simple, practically stateless template, so it modelled in SDL and MSC only service 1, a "generic PUS C service" template and services 6, 9 and 12; the modelling found that the generation of some ST[01] notifications is not properly defined in the standard.129130 The toolset was validated on a Proba-3 use case, which added a housekeeping-compression subservice to service 3 and a mission-specific reaction-wheel management service numbered 8, and was then adopted by two other ESA activities: an R&D project (not flight software) on CoreSight software-execution tracing, which used services 1 and 6 plus a custom service 192, and a TASTE enhancement project whose MSP430 microcontroller demonstrator used services 1, 2, 3 and a custom service 222, with bare-bones encapsulation instead of CCSDS packets to save memory.131132133 Spacebel was also connecting the ASN.1 output to its SRDB tooling, whose database components can be integrated in SCOS-2000, so that on-board software and ground database are consistent by construction.134 Other database tools are listed on MIB tooling.

    Identifiers and notation¶

    Names are for humans and identifiers are for the spacecraft; both are unique within their context, and the widest context is the single spacecraft.22

    Identifier Range in PUS-C Rule
    Service type ID 1-255 standard types at most 127, mission-specific types at least 128; withdrawn standard IDs are not reused23
    Message subtype ID 1-255 standard subtypes at most 127; mission-specific subtypes in a standard service type at least 12824
    Message type ID (service type ID, message subtype ID) uniquely identifies the message type; carried in the packet secondary header24
    Application process identifier (APID) 0-2046 identifies an application process that hosts a provider; 2047 reserved for idle packets, 0 for spacecraft time packets32
    Application process user identifier 0-65535 identifies an application process that hosts a user; used as source ID of requests and destination ID of reports32

    This wiki follows the standard's own notation:

    • ST[xx] - a service type, for example ST[03] housekeeping;7
    • TC[x,y] - a request (telecommand) of service type x and subtype y, for example TC[17,1];
    • TM[x,y] - a report (telemetry), for example TM[1,1] "successful acceptance verification report".45

    A request identifier combines the source ID, the destination APID (plus a system identifier) and the sequence count or packet name; a report identifier combines the source APID, the destination ID and the source sequence count. The standard assumes these are unique for the mission but notes that counter wrap-around or testing can repeat them, so timing information may be needed.3841 Where each identifier lives in the packet is shown on packet structures.

    Messages and transactions¶

    Every message type is either a request type or a report type. A request type defines one or more instruction types and declares whether a request may carry one or several instructions (its "instruction slots"), which instruction types may be combined, and the arguments of each.25 A report type defines exactly one notification type and declares whether one report may hold several notifications.26 Each request type is linked to at most one data report type that serves as its response.29

    Three transaction types use these messages:2837

    • a request-related transaction: one request, zero or more verification reports (depending on the acknowledgement flags and the execution checks), and, if the request asks for data, one or more responses;
    • an autonomous data reporting transaction: a data report generated on the provider's own initiative, for example a periodic housekeeping report;
    • an event reporting transaction: one event report (informative, low, medium or high severity), the event being identified by an event definition within the application process.
    sequenceDiagram
  participant U as Subservice user (ground)
  participant AR as Acceptance and reporting (on board)
  participant P as Subservice provider
  participant ER as Execution reporting
  U->>AR: TC[x,y] request (ack flags 1111)
  AR-->>U: TM[1,1] successful acceptance (TM[1,2] if failed)
  AR->>P: request distributed for execution
  P->>ER: start of execution notification
  ER-->>U: TM[1,3] success (TM[1,4] failure)
  P->>ER: progress of execution notification (per step)
  ER-->>U: TM[1,5] success (TM[1,6] failure)
  P-->>U: data report (response), if the request type has one
  P->>ER: completion notification
  ER-->>U: TM[1,7] success (TM[1,8] failure)

    The diagram combines the request acknowledgement rules of clause 5.4.11.2.2 with the report types of ST[01].3945 Success reports are only sent when the corresponding flag is set; failure reports are always sent. A request whose response would not fit into one telemetry packet of the maximum CCSDS size (65 542 bytes) is rejected; larger transfers are left to file management or mission-specific means.3942

    Request execution verification¶

    When a provider receives a request, it follows a fixed sequence:40

    flowchart TB
  A["Request received by provider"] --> B{"Pre-conditions<br/>fulfilled?"}
  B -- no --> C["Notify failed start of execution<br/>stop processing"]
  B -- yes --> D["Notify successful start"]
  D --> E{"For each step:<br/>execution conditions met?"}
  E -- no --> F["Notify failed progress"]
  E -- yes --> G["Notify successful progress"]
  F --> E
  G --> E
  E -- all steps done --> H{"Any step failed or<br/>post-conditions not met?"}
  H -- yes --> I["Notify failed completion"]
  H -- no --> J["Notify successful completion"]

    A successful completion only means that the provider checked every post-condition in the request type's execution verification profile; it does not necessarily mean that the activity achieved its purpose.40 Each profile lists the condition codes and failure notices (code plus parameters) that can be reported at start, progress and completion.30 Whether failed progress is reported in its own TM[1,6] report or folded into the completion report is declared per request type.36

    Capabilities and applicability constraints¶

    Each subservice type defines at least one capability type, and each capability type carries an applicability constraint that tells a mission whether it must, may or must not provide it:27

    Constraint Meaning Example given by the standard
    minimum every subservice of this type provides the capability the ST[01] verification reports
    by declaration the mission decides when specifying the subservice enabling periodic housekeeping generation
    implied by another capability provided automatically if the other one is disabling periodic housekeeping generation
    by declaration, only if another capability is provided optional, but only on top of another capability register dump on top of register load
    exclusive-or (on a set) at most one of the set CUC or CDS time reports
    inclusive-or (on a set) at least one of the set at least one device access capability

    Annex C of the standard tags every message type with its constraint ("minimum", "by declaration", "requires TC[x,y]" ...).44 PUS-A instead defined, per service, a "minimum capability set" plus "additional capability sets".48 That scheme comes from PSS-07-101, where the minimum set is the simplest level that is still sensible and coherent and must be part of every implementation of the service, and additional sets are optional.8083 The PUS-C scope clause still uses the capability-set wording to describe service levels, but the requirements themselves work with per-capability applicability constraints.3427

    Tailoring: the menu approach¶

    The standard says plainly that it is not meant to be applied in full: the services cover a wide range of scenarios, and the mission picks what it needs. PUS should be viewed as a "Menu" from which services and service levels are selected, separately for each application process.34 Early design choices fix the on-board architecture (number, location and role of application processes) and the services each process supports; missions usually record the result in a space-to-ground interface control document.3 All services are optional, and each can be implemented at several levels, the minimum level being the simplest coherent one.4

    The idea is as old as the PUS. In 1994 PSS-07-101 asked to be viewed as a "Shopping List"; PUS-A and PUS-C use the word "Menu".80484 The 1994 standard also gave three strong recommendations for the selection: de-select requirements and services from the PUS rather than pick them positively, so that each one is considered and dropped only with a sound technical or operational reason; use the standard service whenever the mission needs its function; and implement each service at one of several levels built from "capability sets", the minimum set being included in every implementation. The outcome of the selection was to be recorded in a mission-specific ground/space ICD.80 Field lengths were already a mission choice: the standard fixes only the type of each field, and "mission-optional" fields that belong to capability sets a mission does not implement can be omitted, so a mission can remain compliant without extra packet overhead.9384

    Tailoring the generic level means only adding: mission-specific service types, subservice types, capability types and message types. Reducing the standard capabilities is not recommended because it hampers the reuse of existing hardware and software.31 The standard also assumes that the ground segment implements every standardized capability, so the ground does not constrain tailoring - a point the 2015 presentation repeats: "PUS-C mainly only addresses the on-board capabilities".461

    Mandated interdependencies

    Even a minimal tailoring must respect the dependencies stated by the standard: request verification is accessible to every service in the same application process; event reporting and large packet transfer are accessible to every service; on-board monitoring and event-action need an event reporting service; and an OBCP service that supports observability levels needs one too.20

    Other things a mission must declare include the checksum algorithm used by each provider, the virtual channels used for reports, the on-board memories and their addressing schemes, and the on-board file systems.333435

    ESA's common tailoring: the Generic OIRD¶

    At ESA the operability requirements that drive this tailoring are written by the Agency, for most missions in a mission-specific Operations Interface Requirements Document (OIRD), an early input to spacecraft development, either stand-alone or inside the system requirements document, and tightly coupled with the CCSDS and ECSS standards.103 (XMM already had one in 1994.99) Mission OIRDs used to be derived from earlier ones, so lessons learnt did not always propagate, differences between missions were not always justified, and the OIRDs had evolved independently of PUS-C and the OBCP standard, partly overlapping them; the resulting variability hampered product-oriented on-board software and mission control systems.104 In June 2016 SAVOIR entrusted ESOC with a Generic OIRD (GOIRD) as the baseline for future missions and for compatible on-board software libraries. GOIRD 1.0 was formally released in July 2019 and had already been used for the Copernicus high-priority candidate missions, ERO/Mars Sample Return and HERA.105109

    The GOIRD has two parts, delivered as a PDF document with Excel files (and DOORS modules on request): Part I holds architecture-independent, PUS-agnostic functional requirements traced to the operability standard ECSS-E-ST-70-11C; Part II is a tailoring of PUS-C, written against the PUS-C text in DOORS with a flag (unchanged, modified, deleted or new) and a justification for every change.106107110112 The talk that presented it explains why PUS-C cannot be used as it stands (the GOIRD is also described, with its functional groups and requirement classes, on operations preparation):111

    Size of the full PUS-C, as counted in the GOIRD talk Value
    Service types / subservices 20 / 33, with no mapping to an architecture
    TC and TM message types if every subservice is implemented 316, of which 88 mandatory
    "shall be declared when specifying" statements 382

    These are the talk's own figures. This wiki's count of the clause 8 message type headings gives 318 message types, 81 of them with "minimum" applicability in Annex C (see the message type catalogue); how the talk arrived at 316 and 88 is not stated in the available sources.11144

    Without tailoring two PUS-C implementations would give very different interfaces, and some functions important to ESOC missions were missing (critical event log or system log, file transfer, backup mission timeline).111 The PUS-C part of the GOIRD therefore:113114115116117

    • fixes one common set of services, subservices, capabilities and TC/TM types for all future ESA missions (about 60% of its PUS-C requirements);
    • adds observables to most services, because PUS-C relies heavily on on-demand reports (about 2%);
    • specifies new capabilities at a high level, without TC/TM interfaces - critical event log / system log, parameter extraction from housekeeping, backup mission timeline, file-based operations - to be raised as change requests (about 19%);
    • constrains the avionics architecture: ST[11] is implemented centrally, while services 1, 3, 5, 6 (if applicable) and 17 are required in every packetised unit (under 1%);
    • adds clarifications from earlier missions (about 12%) and changes PUS-C behaviour only minimally, never the defined TC/TM interfaces (under 1%).

    A mission OIRD is then produced from the GOIRD - never by copying a previous mission's OIRD - with only mission-specific deltas, mission constants, the sizing of PUS-C services (for example the capacity of the mission timeline) and the de-scoping of what does not apply.105108 The OBCP standard ECSS-E-ST-70-01C is not tailored; a few extra requirements go into service 18.119 The talk also warns that PUS-C leaves key low-level choices open, such as the number of bytes used to encode certain fields, which matters for interoperability between PUS-C units, and calls for coordination among PUS-C library developers.118 How the 2024 draft compares with some of these points is discussed on PUS-A vs PUS-C.

    How a ground system sees PUS¶

    PUS mainly specifies the spacecraft side, assuming a ground segment that implements every capability.4 In practice the ground knowledge of a mission's PUS tailoring lives in its mission database. In SCOS-2000:

    • incoming telemetry is recognised by APID, service type and subtype - which must sit at the same position in every TM packet of the spacecraft - plus up to two extra identification fields; the combination is looked up in the PID table to find the packet's SPID and structure;64
    • every command definition in CCF carries its service type, subtype, APID and acknowledgement flags, which the header definition copies into the TC packet;65
    • SCOS-2000 decodes and encodes all PUS data types except a few formats (see parameter types), but deduced structures are not supported, and parameters extracted from variable packets cannot feed full TM processing such as limit checks and synthetic parameters, so variable packets should not carry routine housekeeping;6667
    • a few services get ground modelling: service 1 reports drive command verification (using the 4-octet copy of the TC header, and ignoring the failure code), service 9 time reports feed time correlation, and service 11 is modelled with a ground image of the on-board schedule, while most other services get no specific processing.6869 The 4-octet copy is as old as the PUS: the PSS-07-101 verification reports already start with copies of the telecommand's packet ID and packet sequence control, 2 octets each, followed by an optional code and optional parameters.87

    Inference: which PUS the ICD appendix cites

    Appendix C of the ICD points to "Section 23.5 of [AD-2]" for data types, "Section 23.6" for data structures and "Section 6" to "Section 21 of [AD-5]" for services 1 to 19, although its reference list gives [AD-2] and [AD-5] as SCOS-2000 documents (the Commanding SRD and a displays ICD).666951 Those section numbers match ECSS-E-70-41A, where clause 23.5 is "Simple parameter types", 23.6 "Structured field types", clause 6 the telecommand verification service and clause 14 on-board operations scheduling.50 The appendix therefore appears to have been written against PUS-A. The other candidate, ESA PSS-07-101 (which the ICD lists as RD-6 and cites, for example, for the PID packet type and subtype fields),73 also has "simple parameter types" in clause 23.5 and "structured field types" in clause 23.6, but it specifies telecommand verification in clause 5 and has no services 18 and 19, so the service section numbers fit only PUS-A.82

    See also MIB data model, commanding and command verification.

    Reading the standard¶

    ECSS-E-ST-70-41C is long (656 pages) but regular:47

    Part Content
    Clauses 1-4 scope, references, terms, context and the PUS model
    Clause 5 the PUS foundation model (generic service type and service deployment levels)
    Clause 6 system requirements of each service type ST[01]-ST[23]
    Clause 7 space-to-ground interface: packet field types (7.3) and the Space Packet with PUS headers (7.4)
    Clause 8 interface requirements: the layout of every TC[x,y] and TM[x,y]
    Clause 9 the command pulse distribution unit (CPDU)
    Annexes A-D real number formats, CRC and ISO checksum, summary of all message types with applicability, index of clauses per service

    Requirements use "shall", recommendations "should" (to be turned into requirements or tailored out), permissions "may" / "need not", and "can" is descriptive only.43 Clause 6 tells you what a request does; clause 8 tells you how it is encoded, using the PTC/PFC field types of clause 7.3, described on PUS parameter types. The headers of clause 7.4 are described on PUS packet structures, and the differences from the 2003 edition on PUS-A vs PUS-C. The wider ECSS context is on ECSS, ECSS-E-ST-70-11C (space segment operability) and ECSS-E-ST-70-31C (monitoring and control data definition).

    One companion standard is tied to a single PUS service. ECSS-E-ST-70-01C (16 April 2010) specifies on-board control procedures (OBCPs) and the engine that runs them; it assumes that missions with OBCPs also comply with ECSS-E-70-41, whose services support OBCP operation and the interaction with the ground (OBCP operations are initiated by service 18 telecommands), and it maps each OBCP operation onto a service 18 subtype (load 1, delete 2, activate 3, stop 4, suspend 5, resume 6, communicate parameters 7, abort 12).101102 See ST[18].
