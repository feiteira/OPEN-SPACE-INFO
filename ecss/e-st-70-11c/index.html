    ECSS-E-ST-70-11C: space segment operability¶

    ECSS-E-ST-70-11C ("Space engineering – Space segment operability") specifies what on-board functions an unmanned space segment must provide so that it can be operated in flight in any nominal or predefined contingency situation.1 It is the requirements document behind the operator's everyday experience: whether a parameter can be trusted, whether a command is verifiable, whether the spacecraft can survive a week without contact, whether a failed unit can be isolated from the ground. The PUS (ECSS-E-ST-70-41C) says of itself that its services satisfy the fundamental operational requirements for monitoring and control and refers the reader to ECSS-E-ST-70-11 for them.2

    This page follows revision 1 of 15 October 2025, the current issue. Its stated aims are to define operability requirements that let the space segment be operated safely and cost-effectively, ease the preparation, execution and evaluation of check-out and operations, and help suppliers answer requests for proposal. It recognises a cost trade: more operability can raise development cost but certainly lowers operations and maintenance cost.3

    Item Value

    What Rev.1 changed¶

    Rev.1 was driven by experience of modern operations and by the wide adoption of the 2008 issue and of ECSS-E-ST-70-41C; it aligns the scope of the two standards to avoid overlap, prepares for future missions, acknowledges new ECSS and CCSDS standards and applies the ECSS drafting rules. Terminology was partly updated and extended; general and detailed requirements were updated with input from European operators, working groups and ESA's standardised generic operations interface requirements document (the Generic OIRD); clause 5.8 was rewritten to remove overlap with ECSS-E-ST-70-41 and give complementary operability requirements; the mission constants of Annex A were updated; and the tailoring guide of the former Annex B was removed.4 The 2008 issue is not in this wiki's library, so a clause-by-clause comparison is not possible here.

    Terms¶

    The six categories of operability¶

    Category Definition (paraphrased) General requirements

    Commandability Adequate control functions to configure the on-board systems for nominal operations, FDIR and maintenance §4.3

    Critical functions¶

    Term Definition (paraphrased)
    essential telecommand Telecommand distributed without the involvement of on-board software
    high priority command Pulse command routed directly to hardware by a command pulse distribution unit (CPDU)
    high priority telemetry (HPTM) Telemetry that reliably shows the status of mission-critical equipment under all circumstances, normally managed without microprocessor involvement
    locally-critical function Wrong-context execution can degrade local functions temporarily or permanently but not higher-level functionality
    mission-critical function Wrong-context or wrong execution can cause permanent mission degradation
    permanent / temporary degradation of a space segment function The function cannot be achieved on the nominal or any redundant chain for the rest of the mission / for a limited period
    permanent / temporary mission degradation Functions or performance affecting mission products or primary objectives cannot be achieved, for the rest of the mission / for a limited period (for example an outage after a transition to survival mode)
    critical telecommand Telecommand that activates a mission-critical function

    Source: §3.2.2.10 These levels are the "Criticality" values (non critical, locally critical, mission critical) that ECSS-E-ST-70-31C asks suppliers to attach to every activity.11 The communications standard ECSS-E-ST-50C uses "essential telecommand" and "essential telemetry" for the same ideas.12

    Other terms¶

    Among the 35 other definitions, these matter most for ground systems: datation (attaching time to telemetry, including payload data); deferred command (stored in an on-board schedule for later release by time or position); device telecommand (routed to and executed by hardware, such as a relay switch or register load); engineering telemetry (generated to monitor functioning and health) and housekeeping telemetry (cyclic engineering telemetry); parameter (an on-board data item or structure observable via telemetry); parameter validity (the condition under which a parameter's interpretation is reliable, with the example of a gyro output meaningful only when the gyro is powered); report (telemetry aggregated to simplify distribution, storage, transmission and processing); safe mode (autonomously sustainable for a specified period, with permanent communications available for recovery) and survival mode (safety without permanent communications, when safe mode cannot be reached); and telecommand (a self-contained data item from ground with one or more commands; called requests and instructions in the PUS).131415

    Mission constants are quantities the standard cannot fix across missions; they appear in angular brackets, such as <TC_VERIF_DELAY>, and are listed in Annex A. The nomenclature of shall, should, may, need not and can follows ECSS rules; recommendations are expected to become requirements or be tailored out.1617

    General requirements (clause 4)¶

    Clause Requirement (paraphrased)
    4.2 Observability Visibility of internal status, configuration and performance at the detail and delay needed for all routine and specified contingency operations, including diagnosis (contingencies come from the failure analysis, e.g. FMECA)
    4.3 Commandability Monitoring and control functions at every level can achieve the mission objectives in all specified circumstances, using redundancy if needed
    4.4 Compatibility Operations neither constrain nor are constrained by the space–Earth links; the links support all nominal and anticipated contingency operations
    4.5a Safety No telecommand executed at the wrong time or configuration leads to loss of mission (for mission-critical functions, for example, two independent commands such as ARM and FIRE)
    4.5b to e All functions recoverable after a single failure (except agreed single points of failure); no unintentional TC to one function causes failure of another; FDIR overcomes all anticipated failures autonomously or by clear and timely notification; no autonomous reconfiguration leads to an unprotected state (FDIR disabled)
    4.5f to h Operational context data updatable safely and consistently, independently of the current configuration, and partially
    4.6 Flexibility All prime/redundant combinations behave operationally alike; ground allocates units to nominal and redundant chains, can override the live selection and switch reversibly; every function has an alternative configuration with different units; every parameter used by autonomy updatable by TC and visible in telemetry without patch and dump; storage areas should be resizable; remaining lifetime resources (propellant, power, battery, coolant) determinable; resource usage deterministic
    4.7 Testability End-to-end test of any application process (an "are you alive" function); redundant equipment operable off-line; health of unused units should be confirmable before use; redundant memory loadable and checkable; test modes do not disable fault management; no FDIR triggers on off-line data
    4.8 Deactivation Resources to put the space segment in a safe state for the space environment at end of life (de-orbit or graveyard orbit) and to deactivate it completely and permanently (remove energy sources)

    Detailed requirements (clause 5)¶

    Mission level: security and links¶

    The space segment provides security commensurate with mission needs (confidentiality of data streams, authentication and authorization of telecommands). Rev.1 recommends the CCSDS Space Data Link Security protocol (CCSDS 355.0-B, baseline mode for telecommand authentication, Annex E) and the SDLS extended procedures (CCSDS 355.1-B, baseline implementation mode, Annex D), with CCSDS 350.5-G for the concept. Access to telemetry and telecommand must survive specified external influences (typically RFI and weather; meteorites or malicious dazzling clearly cannot be covered) and be recoverable after all specified space and ground configuration changes.2223

    Downlink bandwidth is allocated to classes of data through physical and virtual channels; the assignment of virtual channels to data classes (real-time housekeeping, science, playback, HPTM, critical event log) is agreed with the operator and never changes during the mission, while the bandwidth allocation per VC can be modified; real-time and deferred telemetry can be sent simultaneously.24

    Solar Orbiter shows the pattern. Its frame ICD assigns VC0 to real-time telemetry, VC1 to playback from the on-board computer mass memory, VC2 and VC3 to playback from the solid-state mass memory (the only VCs that may carry compressed packets), VC4 to real-time high priority telemetry, VC5 to critical event log playback and VC7 to idle frames only, with a fixed priority order VC4, VC5, VC0, VC1, VC2, VC3, VC7.25

    Telemetry¶

    Clause 5.3.1 has 35 requirements (a to ii). Grouped by purpose:262728293031

    Purpose Requirements (paraphrased)
    Content Engineering telemetry in all phases gives status, health and performance and lets ground follow nominal and contingency operations independently of command history (a); it includes sensor readings, register readouts, equipment and power status, function status, events and autonomous actions, processor and memory self-tests (b); complete, unambiguous and timely ©; events in dedicated progress or anomaly report packets (d); reporting does not starve other operational information of bandwidth (e)
    Measurement quality Status from direct measurement, not secondary effects, especially for switches (f); switching preconditions acquired independently of the unit to be switched, so a unit can be assessed while off (g); self-contained values, no deltas or status changes (h); fast-changing significant values (pyro currents) memorised (i); adequate range and resolution (j); every mission-critical function observable by at least two independent measurements (k); telemetry to diagnose every FMECA failure down to function or equipment level (l); raw data of on-board processed measurements downlinkable on request (m); units operable in parallel distinguishable, also in events (n); loss of one chain does not block the other's telemetry (o)
    Packet design Every packet with measurements carries what is needed to calibrate and validate them (p); sampling fast enough for all operations (q); all contributing parameters of a given equipment (for synthetic, conditionally valid or deduced parameters) in the same packet ®; same structure and interpretation wherever a parameter appears (s); fixed, implicitly known position in non-variable packets (t); super-commutated parameters sampled regularly (u); no sub-commutation in housekeeping packets (v); one parameter's value in contiguous bits (z)
    Ordering and storage Packets of one APID on one VC delivered in generation order (w); status and health data only in engineering telemetry, since science telemetry is mostly not processed by the control system (x); all packets except idle, time and HPTM packets storable on board until deleted or overwritten (aa); each application process delivers in chronological order (bb); no dynamic ground management of the telemetry configuration in nominal operations (cc)
    HPTM High priority telemetry on the on-board computer, critical latching current limiters and TT&C always available for real-time downlink whenever a transmitter is on, even with application software not running, typically generated by hardware (y)
    Idle data Idle frames on a dedicated VC and only to fill unused bandwidth; idle packets only to complete a frame with meaningful data (dd); telemetry has priority over idle frames and packets, which are not interleaved while data waits (ee)
    Completeness and safe mode Ground can verify completeness of telemetry independently of the route (ff); storage configuration and data preserved in safe mode and on reconfiguration of the storage unit (gg), accessible afterwards with the standard services (hh) and functional without ground intervention (ii)

    Diagnostic mode (5.3.2): ground can select housekeeping parameters for high-rate sampling, generate packets at the sampling rate, sample down to <DIAG_MIN_INTERV> and record diagnostic data on board.32 Compression (5.3.3): missions that compress on board document the compression performance and uncertainty so that ground can predict data volume.32

    Why the packet design rules matter to the ground

    Requirements p, r, s, t and u are what let a ground system define a parameter once and extract it from every packet by position and fixed time offset: in SCOS-2000, one pcf entry per parameter, fixed packet layouts in plf, and sample times computed from the packet time plus a static offset. The SCOS-2000 import ICD states that it relies on exactly these assumptions: housekeeping time offsets must be known a priori and identical for all instances of a packet, and variable packets are excluded from full processing (validity, checks, verification, synthetic parameters).3334

    Telecommanding¶

    Telecommand function (5.5.1, a to u):42434445

    • Telecommands exist for all equipment and functions in all nominal and anticipated contingency conditions, which implies essential telecommands to re-establish command processing after a processor failure (a).
    • One telecommand performs one on-board function, possibly several strictly related actions; loading or starting a schedule counts as one function (b, c). Its action definition never changes during the mission, so no "flip/flop" commands (d); the conditions for configuration-dependent commands are visible in housekeeping (e).
    • No telecommand leads to permanent loss of commanding; repeating a telecommand causes no permanent degradation (f, g). All devices can be commanded individually, not necessarily with high-priority commands (h).
    • Redundancy switching does not change the procedures or commands addressed to the operational unit, so loaded timelines and OBCPs keep working (i). Maximum execution duration is fixed for the mission (j). Parameter values in contiguous bits (k).
    • On-board parameters and tables are changed and read with dedicated commands, not with general memory load and dump (l, m).
    • Distribution at <TC_DISTR_RATE> regardless of source and size, acceptance of <TC_ACC_RATE> commands per second by every application process (n, o).
    • Essential telecommands can be issued directly from the on-board decoder without software (examples: processor module on/off and reset, watchdog enable/disable, reconfiguration module enable/disable, transmitter on/off) on redundant, independent hardware (two decoders), and are identical whatever the commanding route (p to r).
    • Commandable parameters have adequate accuracy, precision and encoding (s). With COP-1 the AD service is configured for the in-orbit conditions, with sliding windows that do not artificially limit the uplink rate (t). Parallel on-board operations do not block ground commanding, for example of the scheduler while it releases commands (u).

    Critical telecommands (5.5.2): they can be implemented through different categories (an OBCP or the schedule executing what is normally a CPDU command, or low-level commands replacing a high-level function); a mission-critical function needs at least two separate command actions (arm or enable, then execute; pyrotechnics are the example); mission-critical register loads have a separate execute command so the loaded data can be verified first; and every mission-critical command has a redundant command on a maximally diverse route with no common nodes.4647

    Transmission and distribution (5.5.3): no restriction when ground commands at the full uplink rate, even with all commands to one application process (for example a memory load), with queues for long-running commands; commanding one application process does not interfere with others; and the source of a telecommand (ground or a named on-board process) is explicit in the packet; commands released from the on-board schedule count as ground-sourced.48

    Verification (5.5.4): complete and unambiguous ground verification of acceptance, routing and execution of any telecommand from any source, by verification reports and housekeeping together, positive and negative, including non-packetised units and essential commands (a); acceptance verification for everything except pure hardware commands such as CPDU commands (b); ground can disable success reports ©; reports within <TC_VERIF_DELAY> of the stage (d); an invalid command (bad length, CRC or APID) fails acceptance and goes no further (e); failures of autonomously generated commands reported as anomaly events (f); execution verifiable from housekeeping generated in all circumstances in which the command runs (g); configuration changes reported in housekeeping (h); effects observable in telemetry available whenever the command can succeed, which for commands affecting telemetry generation means the HPTM (i); register loads echoed by dedicated parameters (j); ground-issued command failures always reported (k); and a command whose effect was already achieved is still positively acknowledged (l).4950

    Modes and configuration¶

    Modes (5.6.1): at least pre-launch, operational and safe modes; modes identified in hardware and software terms and unambiguously visible in periodic housekeeping; any subsystem, unit or instrument put into each mode by a single telecommand function; maintenance possible in nominal modes; mode transitions reported with event reports; any serviceable combination of physical units usable in every mode; forbidden transitions prevented on board but forceable by ground in exceptional cases (for example end-of-life passivation); telemetry storage always possible, in safe mode at least for engineering telemetry; modes available to on-board autonomy; no ground monitoring or commanding needed during launch up to separation, with engineering telemetry stored until ground takes control; transmitters switched on autonomously as soon as permitted; commissioning able to exercise all scenarios; checkouts of payloads and platform items supported.5152

    On-board configuration handling (5.6.2): every reconfiguration ends in a known, observable state, has a deterministic maximum duration and is monitorable at every stage; it does not disturb unrelated units; telemetry gives the cause of the last reconfiguration (or chain of them); units can be pre-configured before selection; safe-state reconfiguration can be triggered autonomously and by ground; after separation the spacecraft enters a state survivable without ground for a predefined period (<GRD_RSP_TIME_CRIT> in critical phases); conflicts between autonomous actions and scheduled commands are avoided or resolved; and power-up, restart or power recovery produces a known, reproducible electrical state.5354

    On-board autonomy and FDIR¶

    General autonomy (5.7.2): autonomy reflects acceptable outage, station coverage and the longest unexpected ground non-availability. The spacecraft continues nominal operations without contact for at least <NOM_OPS_PER> days (for deep space typically 10 days plus the longest conjunction) and stores mission products for that period; after a non-recoverable failure it survives in safe mode, with FDIR active, for at least <SAFE_TIME> days (for deep space at least the longest conjunction plus 10 days). Ground reaction is never needed faster than <GRD_RSP_TIME_NOM> hours in non-critical phases and <GRD_RSP_TIME_CRIT> hours in critical ones. Ground can enable and disable every autonomous function, separately for nominal and safe/survival modes; autonomy can read any engineering telemetry (including event reports from other units) and ground can read every parameter it uses; autonomy uses only valid acquisitions; FDIR never triggers on a single sample and its limits avoid false triggering without compromising safety.555657

    Two autonomy scales are defined:58

    Level Mission execution Functions
    E1 Under ground control; limited on-board capability for safety Real-time ground control; deferred commands for safety
    E2 Pre-planned, ground-defined operations on board On-board scheduler of deferred commands (ST[11])

    Hierarchical FDIR (5.7.5.2b to e): failures are handled at the lowest possible level and handed upwards, every action is reported to ground.60

    Area Requirements (paraphrased)
    General (5.7.5.2) FDIR reflects autonomy, safeguarding and ground intervention needs; hierarchical; escalation to ground as the highest instance; unambiguous reporting with all information for analysis; every FDIR function and each of its inputs can be enabled and disabled by TC (except passive hardware such as fuses), consistently; one failure triggers one surveillance; unit available/failed status managed, reported and updatable by ground; functions disabled by ground stay disabled through mode transitions; no toggling between prime and redundant; no autonomous local reconfiguration of a suspected unit; protection (for example two steps) against unintended modification of FDIR parameters; definitions of all on-board monitorings reportable; statuses of all monitorings and recovery actions in cyclic housekeeping
    Detection (5.7.5.3) Detect and report any anomaly, hardware or software; define out-of-limit conditions on essential parameters (see 5.8.6); no repeated reports of the same failure each cycle; all operationally significant autonomous actions in housekeeping; anomaly reports with unique ID, time and input data record; detection independent of nominal control (for example different AOCS sensors); off-line failure detection where possible; thresholds and repetition counts modifiable by TC; self-check results reported; operational integrity failures reported (late scheduled commands, failed on-board TC files)
    Isolation (5.7.5.4) Isolate failed units; where system safeguarding is at stake (for example a power drain), disable or switch off the offender; otherwise hierarchical steps first (retries and readback, switching with functional verification, delays)
    Recovery (5.7.5.5) Autonomous recovery consistent with mission needs; conflicts with ongoing commanding (schedule, OBCPs) managed; safety guaranteed even if recovery fails; deterministic end configuration

    Sources: §5.7.5.2 to §5.7.5.5.6061626364

    Safe and survival modes (5.7.5.6): safe mode reaches a stable safe condition from any initial state and has an autonomy of at least <SAFE_TIME>; the space segment enters a safe state for any hazard to its health or mission objectives, but enters safe or survival mode only when a system-level critical function is affected; recovery from safe mode is under ground control, after which the return to nominal completes without further ground action; safe mode guarantees permanent uplink and downlink; survival mode is entered when safe mode cannot be reached (solar flare blinding star trackers, corrupted ephemeris, multiple failures), with automatic return to safe mode that ground can enable or disable and that keeps retrying when enabled. Entry includes putting payloads in a safe and recoverable state, transmitting the telemetry needed to recognise the mode (or just the carrier in survival), and keeping the reason for entry available in real time and in storage. Autonomous entry is enabled by default, can be disabled only through a protection mechanism (for example unlock then disable), and its status is always in cyclic telemetry; no nominal operation may need it inhibited or forced. The transition cannot be interrupted (high-priority hardware commanding excepted) and does not rely on residual values from previous modes. Ground recovers within <SM_RECOVERY_TIME> at the lowest safe-mode bit rates; the operational context is in non-volatile memory; the critical event log is returned within <CEL_DUMP_TIME>; downlink starts within <DL_START_TIME>; and a survival mode is required if <MIN_DATA_RATE_SM> cannot be guaranteed in safe mode.65666768

    The PUS-related requirements and the relationship with ECSS-E-ST-70-41¶

    Clause 5.8 assumes and requires that monitoring and control services are supported on board as specified in the PUS, which gives a logical, service-based view of any space segment. Deploying the PUS means choosing the application processes for the physical architecture, the service types to host and, per application process, the subtypes and functions. Clause 5.8 then adds only complementary requirements, the overlap with ECSS-E-ST-70-41 having been removed in Rev.1.694

    5.8.3 Memory Load any changeable area, dump and check any area, no extra size limits per command, integrity during operations, persistent storage (EEPROM); with files, load and dump memory images as files ST[06]

    The dependency runs both ways. ECSS-E-ST-70-41C lists ECSS-E-ST-70-11 as a normative reference and states that its services satisfy the operational requirements found there; the Rev.1 draft of the PUS (DIR1, December 2024) keeps the same wording.27677 According to its change log, that draft also standardises new services, ST[24] file transfer and ST[25] file data storage for file-based operations, ST[26] parameter extraction and ST[27] critical packet log management, and deletes ST[8] function management.78

    Inference

    Linking ST[27] critical packet log management to the critical event log of ECSS-E-ST-70-11C Rev.1 (requirement 5.7.5.6w and VC data class "CEL"), and ST[24]/ST[25] to clause 5.8.11, is this wiki's reading of the two change logs; neither document states the correspondence.

    The criticality flag changed between releases. In the release of 16 April 2020, 79 commands had CCF_CRITICAL = 'Y' (for example ZCSD3404 "Dst-Tx Set Output Power", ZCSF0013 "COMS Execute Recovery Action 1 Function" and ZIC00085 "SDM_OPEN_DOOR"); in the release of 16 September 2025 no command and no command sequence (CSF_CRITICAL) is flagged, although 77 of those 79 command names still exist, now with 'N'.4035 The SCOS-2000 ICD leaves the extra processing or protection of critical commands to mission-specific implementation.98

    Inference

    Why the flag was cleared is not stated in the available sources. Criticality in the 2025 release is presumably handled by other means (procedures, interlocks or two-step command pairs such as the arm commands above), or by mission-specific ground tooling outside the MIB.
