    Space Link Extension (SLE): concept and architecture¶

    Space Link Extension (SLE) is the CCSDS family of ground-to-ground services that carries space link data units (telemetry frames, operational control fields, telecommand CLTUs and packets) between the ground station that terminates the radio link and the mission control centre that actually uses the data. CCSDS defines cross support as one organisation lending part of its space data system to serve another organisation's mission, and SLE is the standard interface where most of that cross support happens: between the tracking stations of one agency and the mission-specific ground systems of another.12137 With SLE, a control centre can bind to a station it does not own, ask for "all frames from this antenna" or "radiate this CLTU at 12:00:03", and receive annotated, time-stamped data over an ordinary terrestrial network, without a mission-specific gateway.3

    This page explains the concept and architecture common to all SLE services. The individual services have their own pages: return services RAF, RCF and ROCF, the forward CLTU service, the Forward Space Packet service, and the wire protocol and software interface, ISP1 and the SLE API. The formal architecture model and the management of agreements and service packages are treated in depth on the cross support reference model and service management; this page summarises what every SLE reader needs from them. In the protocol stack, SLE sits above the TM, AOS, USLP and TC data link protocols and their channel coding and CLTU layers; see the CCSDS overview and the standards map.

    Why SLE exists¶

    The CCSDS space link protocols define how data crosses the radio link. They stop at the ground antenna. A real mission needs the same data units at a control centre, possibly on another continent, possibly hours later, and enriched with facts that only the receiving station knows, such as the reception time. Without standard services, interoperation needs ad hoc gateways designed for each new mission.3 The SLE concept report states the problem plainly: standardising the ground interfaces for transport and management of space data reduces the technical, management and operational cost of cross support.15

    The report lists the expected benefits: agencies can cross-support each other more efficiently; station owners can offer standard services to any CCSDS-compliant spacecraft; data users can reach their payloads through a familiar interface over the internet or ISDN; interfaces can be reused mission after mission; a commercial market for TT&C products is stimulated; and a provider only implements the services it actually needs.3 The ECSS communications handbook makes the same point from the European side. Two CCSDS-compliant implementations may still be unable to interoperate because they chose different options, and the handbook says that SLE has "to a great extent" resolved this problem for ground-segment cross support.16

    CCSDS divides cross support services into three kinds:17137

    Kind Scope CCSDS document part
    SLE services Extend the CCSDS space link services across the ground Cross Support Concept Part 1 (this page)
    Ground communications services The communications that underlie the ground element, for example relaying operational data Part 2 (no plans to produce it in 2006)
    Ground domain services Services that do not use space link data structures: tracking, spacecraft database exchange, mission planning Part 3 (under consideration in 2006)

    The part numbers in this table follow the 2006 concept report and the 2005 reference model, which plans Part 2 for ground communications services and Part 3 for ground domain services.137 The 2015 SLE API books, both the core and the RAF book, number them the other way round (Part 2 Ground Domain Services, Part 3 Ground Communications Services).129165

    The SLE services themselves have two parts. Data transfer services move space link data units between stations, control centres and end users. Management services schedule and provision the transfer services.13 Management happens mostly in a definition phase; data flows in a utilization phase, either in real time or offline with respect to the pass.13

    The cross support reference model¶

    The concept report explains SLE. The normative baseline is a separate Recommended Standard, CCSDS 910.4-B-2, Cross Support Reference Model—Part 1: Space Link Extension Services. It provides the common basis and the reference against which every SLE service specification is written and kept consistent.135 Its framework has four parts: the identification of an SLE system and its environment; an architecture model with a functional view and a management (cross support) view; the common characteristics of SLE services together with a template for their specifications; and the identification of the SLE transfer services, each of which is specified in its own Recommended Standard.135 Ground systems that provide services complying with the SLE specifications are called SLE systems.135

    The model makes four simplifying assumptions: one space mission, one spacecraft, telemetry and telecommand that comply with the CCSDS space link standards, and ground end users that all belong to one mission management entity. Sharing ground systems between missions is not modelled, but it is not ruled out either. Processing of the contents of packet data fields, services that do not transport CCSDS space link data, and the ground communications underneath SLE are all outside the model.136 It is explicitly not a specification or design of a real SLE system.136

    The library copy is issue 2 of October 2005, which added subsections on cross support security and on the time span of agreements and packages to issue 1 of May 1996. Editorial Correction 1 of July 2007 repaired two figures.134 The copy carries the CCSDS "Historical Document" banner, which means the document has either been replaced by a newer issue or withdrawn; which of the two applies is not stated in the available sources.133 The 2015 API books for RAF and FSP still cite issue 2 as a normative reference.167173 The reference model is also older than some of the services it frames. The 2023 RCF and ROCF standards each note that 910.4 defines separate Master Channel and Virtual Channel services, which RCF and ROCF merge, and that a future issue of the reference model is expected to follow.179180

    Vocabulary of the model¶

    The architecture model is object oriented. Its objects have ports, through which they provide services to other objects.139140 Many of the terms used throughout the SLE specifications are defined here:

    Term Definition in the reference model
    Port type Ports involved in providing different services are of different types
    Symmetric / asymmetric ports Symmetric ports each offer all operations of the service; asymmetric ports offer different operations, one acting as consumer and the other as supplier. All SLE services use asymmetric ports.
    Consumer / supplier Direction of data transfer: from the supplier port to the consumer port
    Service user / provider The provider offers a service through its ports. Of two objects, the one closer to the space link is the provider, whatever the direction of data flow
    Invoker / performer One object invokes an operation, the other performs it; most objects do both
    Binding, initiator, responder Two ports of the same type with an association are bound. The initiator requests the binding and the responder completes it
    Physical channel The capability to transfer one stream of bits in one direction
    Space link One forward and/or one return RF carrier, each with one or more physical channels
    Space link session A continuous period in which an SLE system lets one or more physical channels of a space link be used
    SL-DU A data structure defined by the space link standards (frame, packet, CLTU, OCF, ...)
    Space link data channel A uniquely identifiable stream of SL-DUs of one type
    SLE-SDU An annotated SL-DU and/or control information about processing and transfer. In the forward direction an SLE-SDU may carry control alone, for example a COP directive. For the RF signals the SLE-SDU equals the SL-DU
    SLE data channel A uniquely identifiable stream of SLE-SDUs of one type, derived from a space link data channel

    Sources: definitions, environment and data exchange of the reference model.139140141142 Annex A of the model refines the data units. Data-bearing SLE-SDUs carry data, annotation and, possibly, channel status, which reports changes in the condition of the channel. Separate channel status SLE-SDUs exist for the times when no data unit is available to carry the status. SLE-PDUs are either data-bearing (encapsulating SLE-SDUs) or stand-alone protocol control messages. Annotation and channel status remain valid across several user-provider associations, while protocol control information belongs to one association only.143

    The cross support environment¶

    A space data system has a space element (the spacecraft, its payloads and operations subsystems) and a ground element, linked by the space link.18 The ground element contains an SLE System, which extends the transfer of forward and return data between the ground termination of the space link and the Mission Data Operation System (MDOS), the mission-dedicated system that sources forward data and sinks return data. In a mission, the MDOS manages the SLE System.19 The SLE System performs RF modulation and demodulation (RF and ranging), ground termination of the space link protocols, annotation of the data, terrestrial networking, and the interfaces to ground-side protocol processing.19

    The reference model adds precise roles inside the MDOS. Mission User Entities (MUEs) are the sources of forward data and the sinks of return data; one MUE may receive several transfer service instances. The MDOS holds exactly one SLE Utilization Management (SLE-UM) and one or more MUEs, and it may work with several SLE systems at once, harmonising them itself. The SLE-UM manages only what concerns the SLE system, not the whole MDOS or the spacecraft. It is part of the Mission Management Entity, which coordinates space and ground for the mission. The MDOS is a logical model: its MUEs may be spread over several sites or belong to several agencies.141144 In a multi-spacecraft mission each spacecraft is a separate space element.141

    Extension over distance, in information and in time¶

    The space link protocols divide one physical channel into logical space data channels (for example virtual channels), each carrying frames or packets. SLE gives ground users access to these channels and extends them in three ways:22

    Extension Meaning Example from the concept report
    Over distance Protocol processing may happen at sites far from the antenna. Each site must forward enough context for the next site to finish processing, because space link protocols are not strictly layered. The APID is only unique within a spacecraft; it is qualified by the SCID in the frame header, so a site that extracts packets far from the frame processing site needs the SCID too.23
    In information (annotation) Facts known only at reception are added to each data unit. Earth reception time, receiving location, data unit quality, sequence continuity; also data type, operations mode, and format parameters such as frame size.24
    In time Data can be delivered later than they were received, from a brief delay (a reduced ground communication rate) to a long one (temporary storage and later forwarding). Temporary storage at a processing facility followed by later packet extraction.2526

    Information that is not tied to a single data unit, such as "loss of frame synchronization", must also be conveyed, and each service specification defines it.23 On the return services page you can see exactly which annotation fields the RAF, RCF and ROCF services carry.

    Functional groups and SLE complexes¶

    SLE describes processing with SLE Functional Groups (SLE-FGs). An SLE-FG is a building block derived from the layered functionality of channels, frames and packets in the space link protocols. It cannot be decomposed further, and it represents the set of SLE services that a real system can implement at that layer.27 A physical facility that implements a set of functional groups is an SLE Complex. All functions of one functional group must sit in one complex, but different groups can be spread over complexes run by different organisations.20 A complex has a service provision component and a management component, and it is one collection of resources under a single management authority: one station, a network of stations, or, for packet services delivered to payload control centres, a spacecraft control centre.2829

    The reference model makes this formal. There are exactly eight SLE-FG types in three categories: three return SLE-FGs, two forward AOS SLE-FGs and three forward telecommand SLE-FGs.145 Each SLE-FG has transfer ports of two kinds, Transfer User (T-U) and Transfer Provider (T-P), and exactly one Function-Management Provider port (FM-P) for set-up and configuration. A T-U port is bound to a T-P port of the same type on another SLE-FG or on the space element. A T-P port is bound to a T-U port of another SLE-FG or of an MUE. Every service instance uses its own pair of port instances.145 At the complex level, an SLE complex exposes at least one user and one provider transfer port, plus a single Complex Management port bound to SLE Utilization Management. A real system that supports several missions exposes one Complex Management port per mission. A complex need not expose every SLE-FG port: for a Return Space Packet service produced from the RF signal, several SLE-FGs are cascaded inside the complex, and only the RF input and the packet output are visible outside.146 Complex Management negotiates service packages with SLE-UM, distributes set-up and configuration parameters to its SLE-FGs, and collects and consolidates their reports.146

    Ports and operation types¶

    In the reference model an SLE service is described by the set of operations that can be invoked and performed through one bound pair of ports of the same type.147 Each SLE service is provided between exactly one port instance of one object and one port instance of another.140 The model names four generic operation types for transfer ports. Who invokes each one depends on the direction, because the roles of supplier and consumer swap:147149150

    Operation type Purpose Return port (provider = supplier) Forward port (provider = consumer)
    Transfer_Data Carries an SLE-SDU with space link data invoked by the provider invoked by the user
    Transfer_Set Initialises or updates the service attributes and configuration options of the user's instance invoked by the user invoked by the user
    Transfer_Get Retrieves status and configuration of the instance invoked by the user invoked by the user
    Transfer_Notification Reports status (periodic or on event) and accounting information invoked by the provider invoked by the provider

    Port-type-specific operation names append the type to the port name, for example Rtn-Bit-TransferData or Fwd-Bit-TransferSet.149150 Two further port types face the space element and have no user or provider in this sense: Rtn-RF-Transfer (the space element supplies the RF signal) and Fwd-RF-Transfer (the SLE system supplies it), each with a single ...TransferData operation.150

    Inference

    The concrete operations of today's specifications fit these types: TRANSFER-DATA (and the RAF transfer buffer) is a Transfer_Data, START and SCHEDULE-STATUS-REPORT are Transfer_Set operations, GET-PARAMETER is a Transfer_Get, and SYNC-NOTIFY, ASYNC-NOTIFY and STATUS-REPORT are Transfer_Notification operations. The reference model does not make this mapping, and it describes binding (BIND, UNBIND) as a relation between ports rather than as one of the four types.

    Management ports follow the same pattern. Each SLE-FG supplies one management port, through which Complex Management configures and monitors production. The SLE-FG (supplier) invokes Management_Notification; Complex Management (consumer) invokes Management_Set and Management_Get.151

    Service provision and service production¶

    Every SLE transfer service specification separates two activities:4142

    • Service production transforms the physical channel into the SLE data channel (return), or the SLE data channel into the RF carrier (forward). For RAF this means acquiring the carrier, demodulating, synchronising, decoding and checking frames; for FCLTU it means modulating and radiating CLTUs.
    • Service provision makes the operations available to the user: when service is provided (start and stop times), how (user- or provider-initiated binding), and with which quality of service (for example whether frames with known bit errors are delivered).43

    The distinction decides who may change what. Provision parameters affect a single service instance; service management initialises them, and the user may change some of them through service operations. Production parameters such as bit rate, frame length, coding type or modulation index can affect several service instances or the complex's resources, so only service management may change them.4142 One RAF production instance can feed several RAF service instances.43 FCLTU adds a controlled exception: if agreed, the user can ask complex management to change production parameters with the CLTU-THROW-EVENT operation.42

    Three kinds of data unit appear in every specification:43

    • the SL-DU (space link data unit), for example a telemetry frame as acquired from the link;
    • the SLE-SDU (SLE service data unit), the SL-DU plus its annotation, or a notification. SLE-SDUs are the parameters of the TRANSFER-DATA and SYNC-NOTIFY operations;
    • the SLE-PDU (SLE protocol data unit), the encoded invocation or return of an operation. Usually one PDU carries one operation, but return services pack many TRANSFER-DATA and SYNC-NOTIFY invocations into one transfer buffer PDU.

    Service instances¶

    A service instance is the provision by one SLE complex of the capability to transfer one or more SLE data channels of one type, all related to the same space link session.28144 The provision of RAF from one physical channel to one user is one instance; the same channel served to two users, or several channels served concurrently, are several instances.50 For FCLTU only one instance may exist per physical forward channel at a time.51

    Each instance has:

    • a service instance provision period, the time during which it is scheduled to be provided. Reaching its start is the event 'start of service instance provision period' in the state tables;52
    • a binding direction: user-initiated (only the user may invoke BIND) or provider-initiated (only the provider may). Service management decides which, per instance. RAF, RCF and ROCF allow both; FCLTU-BIND is invoked only by the user;5354
    • a delivery mode (above);
    • a responder port identifier, a logical name agreed by both sides in advance and translated locally into technology-specific addresses, for example an IP address and TCP port;45
    • a service instance identifier that is unique within the providing complex.55

    The service instance identifier¶

    The service instance identifier is a sequence of attribute name/value pairs. In its standard string form, each pair is written name=value and pairs are separated by dots. The attributes, in order, are the service agreement (sagr), the service package (spack), the forward or return functional group (fsl-fg or rsl-fg) and the service name. For return services the service name value starts with onlc, onlt or offl followed by an instance number; for forward services it is the service name plus a number, such as cltu1.56 The standard's own example is sagr=xyz.spack=abcdef.rsl-fg=gfjdy.raf=onlc2.57

    Inference

    The ASN.1 comments list the prefixes onlc, onlt and offl without expanding them. Reading them as online complete, online timely and offline matches the three delivery modes, and ISP1 Annex B uses onlt1 for "an RCF service instance with timely online delivery mode",59 but the 911.x specifications do not define the prefixes explicitly.

    Each attribute is also registered as an ASN.1 object identifier under {iso 3 112 4 3 1 2}: for example raf is arc 22, rcf 46, rocf 49, cltu 7, fsp 10, sagr 52 and spack 53.58

    Operations common to all transfer services¶

    All SLE transfer services are built from a small vocabulary of operations. An operation is invoked by one object and performed by the other; a confirmed operation returns a report of its outcome, an unconfirmed one does not.62 The common operations are:63

    Rules that apply to every operation¶

    • Result and diagnostic. Every return carries a result (positive or negative). A negative result carries a diagnostic; the value 'other reason' may only be used when nothing more specific applies. Checks should be done in the order in which the diagnostics are listed, and an implementation that deviates must document its order.66
    • Parameter checking. A parameter is invalid if it is outside the range permitted by service management, conflicts with another parameter of the same invocation (for example a start time after the stop time), or conflicts with the current configuration. Invalid operations are not performed.67
    • Blocking and non-blocking. Every confirmed operation except BIND and UNBIND carries an invoke-ID, an arbitrary 16-bit integer (InvokeId ::= IntUnsignedShort) returned unchanged by the performer. Operations with an invoke-ID are non-blocking: the invoker may issue more invocations before the returns arrive. BIND and UNBIND are blocking. Reusing an invoke-ID that is still awaiting a return is an error; an invoker that wants strictly sequential behaviour can simply use a constant invoke-ID. Effects must be as if operations were performed in the order received.6869
    • Return timeout. If a confirmed operation's return does not arrive within the return-timeout-period (1 to 600 seconds in the ASN.1), the invoker aborts the association with PEER-ABORT 'return timeout'. Returns must therefore never depend on human interaction.7071
    • Protocol errors. An operation not allowed in the current state causes PEER-ABORT 'protocol error'; an undecodable PDU causes PEER-ABORT 'encoding error'; a return with an unknown invoke-ID causes PEER-ABORT 'unsolicited invoke-ID'.72
    • Communications failure. Each bound entity must monitor the health of the communications link, if necessary with a periodic heartbeat, and treat a disruption as a 'protocol abort'. After a protocol abort the provider returns to 'unbound', discards its transfer buffer and resets parameters to their initial values, but keeps the statistics for status reports.73
    • Time. All times are UTC. Return services express the earth receive time in the CCSDS Day Segmented code with a 1958-01-01 epoch and a 16-bit day field, at microsecond or picosecond resolution, accurate to 1 ms; FCLTU times must be precise and accurate to 0.1 s.7475 See time code formats.

    BIND, UNBIND and PEER-ABORT in detail¶

    These three operations are common to all services and share one ASN.1 module (CCSDS-SLE-TRANSFER-SERVICE-BIND-TYPES).80

    BIND carries the invoker's credentials, the initiator-identifier (an authority identifier of 3 to 16 visible characters), the responder-port-identifier (a logical port name of 1 to 128 characters), the service-type, the version-number and the service-instance-identifier. The return carries the performer's credentials, the responder-identifier, and either the accepted version number or a diagnostic.8180 The responder port identifier is in the invocation only to help gateways; the responder ignores it when validating the BIND. A wrong value usually makes the BIND fail with no return at all, which ends in a 'return timeout' abort.82 The service type is redundant today, but it anticipates a future generic SLE-BIND.83

    Version negotiation works downwards only. The responder either accepts the proposed version or, if it supports negotiation, proposes a lower one; if it supports neither, it rejects the BIND with 'version not supported'. An initiator that cannot accept the lower version unbinds. The 2023 issues of RAF, RCF, ROCF and FCLTU all define version number 6.84858687

    Security: access control and authentication¶

    SLE transfer services build in two security mechanisms, access control and authentication, and leave everything else (confidentiality, integrity, availability, auditing) to the underlying communications or to the mission's own end-to-end measures.9293

    Access control works at two levels: the initiator and responder must be registered with each other, and they must be authorised for the specific service instance. Service management specifies the authorised initiator and responder of each instance, and each side presents its identity in the BIND.94 The responder refuses an unknown initiator with 'access denied' and does not even try to authenticate it; a known but unauthorised initiator gets 'service instance not accessible to this initiator'. The initiator, in turn, aborts if the responder identity in the BIND return is unknown ('access denied') or not the expected one ('unexpected responder ID').95

    Authentication uses credentials that rely on a secret shared by user and provider. Each instance has one of three authentication levels:9697

    Level What carries credentials
    'all' Every invocation and return except the PEER-ABORT invocation
    'bind' Only the BIND invocation and its return
    'none' Nothing; authentication left to the environment

    Providers should require 'all' or 'bind'. The two management sides agree the level and the algorithm, and give both ends the passwords or keys.97 In ASN.1, credentials are either unused or an octet string of 8 to 256 octets whose internal structure depends on the algorithm.98 A PDU whose credentials fail authentication is silently ignored, as if it had never arrived.99 The algorithm actually used over TCP/IP is the ISP1 hashed password scheme, described on the ISP1 page.

    The concept report models the configuration as entity authentication structures (local and peer entity identifiers, each with an authentication level, method and method parameters) and service instance structures (service instance identifier, initiator identifier, responder identifier, responder port identifier).100

    The specifications are frank about the residual risks. Without additional mechanisms an attacker could read telemetry or commands as they cross the wide area network, or replace the data parameter of a PDU. FCLTU adds one integrity check of its own (the monotonically increasing cltu-identification), but the FCLTU standard warns that a hijacked forward service could be used to subvert or destroy a spacecraft.101102 These pages describe the standards' protections; they do not discuss attack techniques.

    SLE service management¶

    SLE service management collects and exchanges the information needed to agree, schedule, prepare and access SLE services. It is a dialogue between Utilization Management (UM) in the MDOS and Complex Management (CM) in each SLE complex.103 CM presents the complex in a standard way and hides its internal equipment from the user; CCSDS does not define how CM drives that equipment.104 Of the five OSI management functional areas, SLE service management covers configuration, accounting, fault and security management, but not performance management, which stays internal to the provider.105

    UM requests provision periods, provides RF, modulation, space link and SLE configuration, provides trajectory predictions and coordinates the mission user entities. CM negotiates service types, numbers of instances and agreement durations, answers requests for space link sessions, and configures and monitors the complex's resources.106

    Agreements, packages and provision periods¶

    The reference model fixes the vocabulary for how long each management object lives:161162163

    • An SLE Service Agreement states which transfer and management services one SLE complex provides to one mission, and on what conditions. Only one agreement is in force between a given complex and a given mission at any time, but a mission may have several over its lifetime. The agreement period often covers the spacecraft's whole operational life, with several packages a day and hundreds or thousands in all; it can also be short, for example launch support, one orbit manoeuvre or an emergency.
    • An SLE Service Package is the set of transfer service instances, with the specification of their production, that one complex provides to one or more users. Its lifetime has a definition phase and a utilization phase. A package has at most one online period, tied to one space link session. Its offline instances may serve that session or sessions of other packages.
    • The service instance provision period is the time during which one instance's capability is provided. Online instances may start before and end after their session.

    During the definition phase, SLE-UM creates a servicePackage managed object at the complex, together with the lower-level managed objects it contains, and chooses parameter values within the limits of the agreement. The attributes of these objects select the transfer services, identify the interfaces where they are offered and the services that this or other complexes must provide, carry the mission-specific information Complex Management needs to configure production, name the eventual users (MUEs or other complexes), schedule the online and offline instances, and define the reporting from Complex Management to SLE-UM.163 During utilization, provision is controlled in two ways: MUEs invoke control operations on their instances, and SLE-UM invokes management operations. Debrief reports may follow any provision period.163 When a mission uses two complexes, it holds two agreements and negotiates a package with each; each package then specifies only the part of production that its complex performs.161144

    What is and is not in this library

    The reference model (CCSDS 910.4-B-2) is now in the library and is summarised above. The normative service management specification (CCSDS 910.11), which all SLE documents cite, is not. The newer extensible cross support service management documents and their data formats are described on the cross support reference model and service management.

    The SLE API and ISP1 in one paragraph¶

    The transfer service specifications define operations and ASN.1 types in an abstract way. Two further documents make them implementable. ISP1 (CCSDS 913.1) maps one SLE association to one TCP connection, encodes PDUs with ASN.1 BER, frames them in 8-octet-header TML messages, adds a heartbeat, and defines the hashed-password credentials.115 The SLE API (CCSDS 914.0) defines C++ components (API Proxy, API Service Element, SLE Operations, SLE Utilities) that hide the communications technology and enforce the state tables, so that SLE applications can be written once and products can be exchanged.10116 Service-specific API books add the classes, interfaces and checks of one service each; the RAF book (915.1-M-2), for example, adds the RAF service instance classes and operation objects, and the FSP book (916.3-M-2) adds FOP monitoring for the provider side.170174 Both layers are explained on ISP1 and the SLE API.

    How ESA's ground segment uses SLE¶

    In ESA's ground segment the mission control system (SCOS-2000) does not talk to stations directly. A 2005 ESOC overview describes the NCTRS (Network Control and TM/TC Router System) as the interface between the mission control system and the ground station network, using SLE for non-ESA stations.117 A 2003 ESOC presentation shows the NCTRS between SCOS-2000 and the stations (Kiruna, Redu, Perth, Villafranca, Kourou, New Norcia, Cebreros and the DSN), labels the link SLE, and lists SLE-conformant ground station interface software among SCOS-2000's enabling technologies.118119 The page on NCTRS, NIS and the ground station interface covers these systems.

    Several sources in this library show traces of SLE in ESA's data products and databases:

    • Command verification. The EDDS external user ICD names the first command verification stage 'R', release from SCOS-2000 to "the SLE User (NCTRS/NIS)", and defines 'G' (ground station reception) and 'T' (uplink, also called radiation; for throw event commands it is the execution stage).120 See command verification in the MIB.
    • Packet headers. The Rosetta and Mars Express data delivery ICD defines an 8-bit "SLE Type" field in its 18-octet DDS packet header. It encodes the SLE channel (SLC, MC, VC, bad frames) and the delivery flavour (real-time, real-time flow control, off-line) as values 0 to 11.121 EDDS keeps an 8-bit SleServiceID in its raw packet header "for backward compatibility with the GDDS".122 See EDDS formats.
    • The MIB. The SCOS-2000 database import ICD defines command type CCF_CTYPE = 'T' for SLE throw events, sent to the SLE service provider but not uplinked, and 'N' for NIS throw events, executed by the NIS itself.123 The Solar Orbiter MIB contains six NIS throw events and no SLE throw events (queries below).60

    Outside ESA's own network, SCOS-2000-based centres reach SLE stations through protocol bridges. At DLR's German Space Operations Center, the SCOS-2000-based GECCOS system speaks the NCTRS protocol to an SLE Switch Board (SSB), which translates it into the standard SLE protocol used by ground stations worldwide.124125 ESA's newer EGS-CC framework includes reference components to talk to a PUS spacecraft via SLE, and plans an extension for CSTS monitoring data.12640 See ancillary systems and EGS-CC. The ECSS communications handbook points to the ESA ground operations software portal, which includes "the ESA SLE Gateway".127 ESA's role in the SLE API is described on the ISP1 and SLE API page.

    Two open-source Java implementations are public on GitHub (web sources, not in this library). ESA's SLE Java API (esa/sleapi-j) describes itself as an SLE API package supporting RAF (911.1), RCF (911.2), ROCF (911.5), FCLTU (912.1) and FSP (912.3).183 jsle (yamcs/jsle), from the Yamcs project, implements the user and provider sides of FCLTU, RAF and RCF.184 Their details are on the ISP1 and SLE API page.

    In the Solar Orbiter databases of this library, the NIS throw events and the ground-generated packets exist only in the spacecraft MIB.
