    Space Packet Protocol (CCSDS 133.0-B-2)¶

    The Space Packet is the basic data unit of almost every CCSDS-compliant spacecraft. Every housekeeping report, event message, memory dump and telecommand that the SCOS-2000 control system handles for an ESA mission travels in one. The Space Packet Protocol (SPP) defines that data unit and two simple services that carry it. The packet starts with a fixed 6-octet header that names the data stream (the APID), says whether the packet is telemetry or a telecommand, counts packets, and gives the length. Everything after that header belongs to the user application.57 The protocol is deliberately small. Its rationale is that a single, common data structure for variable-length application data can be created, stored on board, sent over one or more space links and handled in ground systems.2 The CCSDS Green Book Space Packet Protocols (130.3-G-1, April 2023) explains the concept from the users' side, together with the Encapsulation Packet Protocol, and gives guidance on choosing between them; it is informative, and the Blue Books prevail where they differ (Green Book rationale).78

    This page follows CCSDS 133.0-B-2 (June 2020, with Editorial Changes 1 and 2), the issue held in the library.1 For the wider picture, see the CCSDS protocol stack overview. For the PUS fields carried inside the packet, see PUS packet structures.

    Property Value

    Where the protocol sits¶

    The CCSDS reference model shows SPP twice. At the top it is an Application Layer protocol whose packets carry user data directly. Lower down it is a "shim" that can carry the data units of other protocols, a role it shares with the Encapsulation Packet Protocol. Editorial Change 1 of 2020 corrected the figure to make clear that the choice between EPP and SPP in that shim position is exclusive.51 The 2023 Overview of Space Communications Protocols also lists SPP among the CCSDS Application Layer protocols.31

    The Green Book draws the same stack with Bundle Protocol, TCP/UDP over IP, LTP and IP over CCSDS (IPoC) as the upper layers that use the shim, and notes that the synchronization and coding sublayer is not shown. Its definitions stress that neither SPP nor EPP is a Network Layer protocol.8179 Asked whether the packet protocols can coexist with Internet technologies and Delay Tolerant Networking (DTN), it answers "a resounding yes". Telemetry and telecommand are Application Layer activities that may use Space Packets; DTN or Internet protocols may carry those packets across subnetworks where they give the required end-to-end performance; and either packet protocol may sit as the shim under DTN, CFDP, LTP or IP. IP datagrams themselves travel one-for-one in Encapsulation Packets, using the Internet Protocol Extension convention of IPoC.88

    SPP defines no path, network or routing function and has no networking capability of its own. Every forwarding decision depends on the subnetworks underneath and on the mission's own meaning for each APID.5 Over a space link the packets ride on the Packet Services of the Space Data Link Protocols (TM, TC, AOS, Proximity-1 and USLP) or on the Bundle Protocol. SPP can also run between on-board instruments and data stores over the SOIS Packet Service, over local message buses, and over terrestrial TCP/IP sockets.12

    The managed data path¶

    Before any packet flows, design or management has to configure the route it takes from source to destination. CCSDS calls this route a managed data path, and only the management system can reconfigure it. A managed data path has one source end system, one or more destination end systems, one or more subnetworks and, where several subnetworks are involved, the intermediate systems that join them. The only help SPP gives for routing is the APID.63

    The 2023 overview makes the same point from the network side. At each relay, some mechanism outside SPP looks at the APID and forwards the packet according to its instructions. There is no end-point address and no interoperable forwarding mechanism. On the ground, Space Packets are usually moved with a Space Link Extension service.33 The Green Book adds two points. The behaviour of intermediate nodes is implementation-specific and outside the scope of the report, and if a packet has several destinations, SPP entities at the source or at intermediate systems may multicast it.82 Store-and-forward on board is also a management action within the managed data path: an instrument that produces data out of contact need not know whether its packets go to the ground at once or wait in an on-board store.86

    APID naming domains¶

    An APID is unique only within one naming domain, which usually corresponds to one spacecraft or one element of a constellation. Each project allocates the APIDs in its own domain. A mission with several processors, a "fractionated" spacecraft or several spacecraft must either share out the one APID domain or extend it with mission-specific fields in the packet secondary header.85 Missions may use the APID to mean the source, the destination or the type of a packet stream. CCSDS leaves that choice to the mission.5 The Green Book adds that frame-level mechanisms such as virtual channel multiplexing over a master channel can also help, that CCSDS defines no standard APID domain naming service, and that it sees the endpoint identifiers of DTN, which are URIs naming source and destination, as the better long-term approach to enterprise-wide addressing.8190

    Service characteristics¶

    Both SPP services are preconfigured, unidirectional, asynchronous, unconfirmed, incomplete and non-sequence-preserving:9

    Characteristic What it means for a user
    Preconfigured Data moves only along a managed data path set up in advance by management.
    Unidirectional One end sends and the other receives. Two-way exchange needs two managed data paths.
    Asynchronous No timing rules, although the provider may limit the generation rate.
    Unconfirmed The sender gets no acknowledgement from the receiving end.
    Incomplete Neither completeness nor retransmission is guaranteed.
    Non-sequence-preserving The order of SDUs may change along the path.

    SDUs given to one SAP are processed in the order they were submitted. No order is kept between different SAPs. CCSDS does not define flow control between user and provider. SPP has no quality-of-service mechanism of its own, so end-to-end performance depends on the subnetworks along the path.9

    The protocol entity does three things. It generates or validates the header that identifies the data. It starts the transfer through the subnetworks. It multiplexes and demultiplexes several managed data paths onto one subnetwork connection. It explicitly does not set up or release connections, segment SDUs, retransmit, control flow, or provide quality-of-service features.11

    Why a Space Packet? The Green Book rationale¶

    The Green Book 130.3-G-1 was written to give an introductory overview of SPP and EPP, to show end users and infrastructure developers how to apply them on board and on the ground, and to explain the distinctions between the two so that implementers choose correctly. It is not part of either Recommended Standard.78 It describes the Space Packet as a self-delimited carrier whose APID identifies the content, source or user of the data within an enterprise; parameters such as time, and the format of the contents, are identified in the mission context through the APID.81 Packets are variable in length, or fixed if the user wishes, and are sent at variable intervals; apart from the primary header the content is entirely under the control of the user application.82

    Independence and flexibility¶

    The report names two benefits. Independence: before the Space Packet existed, many on-board activities had to be synchronized with the generation of telemetry frames, and instruments on the same spacecraft had to coordinate their telemetry rates and timing. The packet hides those mechanisms, so instrument developers can design their applications almost independently of the transfer mechanism and of the other instruments, and applications can be reused between projects that use different subnetwork technologies, which the report expects to reduce development cost. Flexibility: within the resources that project management allocates, an application can send any kind of data (commands, operation plans, housekeeping, science, memory loads and dumps) at the rate and time it chooses, and images of different sizes can be compressed freely.84

    Unconfirmed, incomplete, one-way¶

    The protocol confirms nothing, does not retransmit and may not preserve order. A user may add an error detection code to the application data to check that the packet survived transport. Where confirmation, retransmission or ordering is needed, the applications must provide it; a common practice is for the destination to send a confirmation back over a managed data path in the opposite direction when it has received important data such as commands. CCSDS has no standard for doing this with SPP, but its LTP and CFDP protocols give reliable transfer above it. Simpler missions may rely on operator retransmission, on the data link layer, or on sending the same data several times.85 Each managed data path is one-way, so commands and telemetry use separate paths; the report argues that this costs little, because command and telemetry flows are rarely symmetric and far more applications receive telemetry from a spacecraft than send it commands.85

    Inference

    The PUS fills exactly these gaps on ESA missions: the packet error control field is the optional error-detection code inside the application data, and the ST[01] verification reports are the confirmations sent back on a telemetry path, matched to the request by a copy of its packet ID and sequence control.5152 The Green Book does not mention PUS; the mapping is this wiki's.

    The report's answers on reliability and timing complete the picture. Neither SPP nor EPP can recover a missing packet; packets become reliable point to point when carried in frames protected by COP-1 (telecommand frames) or COP-P (Proximity-1 frames), or end to end with CFDP, LTP, TCP over IPoC, or the application itself. Neither protocol guarantees a minimum delay, but real-time data can be sent on high-priority Virtual Channels, the SPP or EPP entities may order packets according to the quality of service of each managed data path, and isochronous data such as voice or launch-vehicle telemetry can use the Insert Zone of AOS or USLP.89

    • Path 2 carries one command per packet for real-time control, or one observation plan per packet when the instrument works autonomously.
    • Path 3 carries periodic status, one set per packet; it can also return an acknowledgement for each command received on path 2 so that the control system can resend lost ones.

    Inference

    In Solar Orbiter terms, path 2 corresponds to the telecommand APIDs (packet category 12), path 3 to housekeeping and the service-1 verification reports (categories 4 and 1), and path 1 to private science or file transfer (categories 12 and 10) described in the case study below. The Green Book example is generic and does not refer to any mission.73

    The two services¶

    Service SDU SAP address SDU size
    Packet Service Space Packet, already formatted by the user APID 7 to 65 542 octets
    Octet String Service Delimited octet string; SPP builds the packet APID 1 to 65 536 octets

    Service summary after CCSDS 133.0-B-2 table 2-1 and clause 3.2.1013

    Projects may set lower maximum lengths, taking into account the largest SDU that every subnetwork on the path can carry.13 The two ends of a path need not use the same service type. An Octet String request at the source may be delivered through the Packet Service at the destination. In the Packet Service the Space Packet is both the SDU and the PDU.10

    Packet Service¶

    The user hands over a complete Space Packet built according to clause 4.1, and the provider moves it unchanged.14

    Primitive Parameters Notes
    PACKET.request Space Packet, APID, QoS Requirement (optional) Asks the provider to send the packet.
    PACKET.indication Space Packet, APID, Packet Loss Indicator (optional) Delivers the packet. The effect on the user is undefined.
    • APID is mandatory. It identifies the source, destination or type of the packet, and its meaning is mission-specific.14
    • Packet Loss Indicator is optional. It tells the receiving user that a gap in the Packet Sequence Count shows one or more packets were lost. An implementation that provides it must declare it at design time and use it the same way on all sides.14
    • QoS Requirement is optional. It selects a quality-of-service level when a subnetwork offers several. Over the TC Space Data Link Protocol it chooses between Type-A (sequence-controlled) and Type-B (expedited) transfer of each packet.14

    Octet String Service¶

    The user supplies an octet string. The provider builds exactly one Space Packet for each OCTET_STRING.request, using managed parameters that include either a Packet Sequence Count or a Packet Name.15

    Primitive Parameters
    OCTET_STRING.request Octet String, APID, Secondary Header Indicator, Packet Type, Packet Sequence Count/Packet Name
    OCTET_STRING.indication Octet String, APID, Secondary Header Indicator, Data Loss Indicator (optional)

    The Secondary Header Indicator tells the provider whether the octet string begins with a Packet Secondary Header, and the provider copies it into the Secondary Header Flag.15 When the Packet Type is 1 (telecommand), the request also carries the sequence count or packet name.15 An octet string always goes into the User Data Field of a single packet. The service cannot segment, so the Sequence Flags are always 0b11 wherever the Octet String Service is used on a path.1319

    The Space Packet¶

    A Space Packet is a Packet Primary Header of 6 octets followed by a Packet Data Field of 1 to 65 536 octets. The data field holds an optional Packet Secondary Header and/or a User Data Field, and at least one of the two must be present.1621

    Field Bits (position) Values Meaning and rationale
    Packet Version Number 3 (bits 0 to 2) 0b000 Marks the data unit as a Space Packet. The field leaves room for other packet structures, such as the Encapsulation Packet with PVN 0b111.1730
    Packet Type 1 (bit 3) 0 TM, 1 TC Separates telemetry (reporting) packets from telecommand (requesting) packets. The project defines what counts as each.18
    Secondary Header Flag 1 (bit 4) 0 or 1 Shows whether a Packet Secondary Header is present. It is fixed per APID and managed data path for a Mission Phase, and it is 0 for Idle Packets.18
    Application Process Identifier 11 (bits 5 to 15) 0 to 2047 Names the managed data path. 2047 (all ones) is reserved for Idle Packets. Other values need not be consecutive.18
    Sequence Flags 2 (bits 16 and 17) 0b00, 0b01, 0b10, 0b11 Continuation, first or last segment of a larger set of user data, or unsegmented. Users need not use them.19
    Packet Sequence Count or Packet Name 14 (bits 18 to 31) 0 to 16 383 TM packets carry a count per APID. TC packets carry either a count or a Packet Name, which may be any 14-bit pattern.19
    Packet Data Length 16 (bits 32 to 47) 0 to 65 535 Length count C, equal to the number of octets in the Packet Data Field minus one.20

    The bits of every field are numbered from the first one transmitted, which is bit 0. Where a field holds a number, bit 0 is the most significant bit. Octets are numbered from zero, and spare bits are always 0.4

    Packet Version Number¶

    The three PVN bits are always 0b000. This identifies the "Version 1 CCSDS Packet", the name the standard gives the Space Packet.17 The PVN matters at the data link layer. The TM, TC and AOS protocols carry directly only packets whose PVN CCSDS has authorized. A receiving entity uses the PVN to hand each extracted packet to the right user.3239 Protocol data units without an authorized PVN reach the link through the Encapsulation Packet Protocol.32

    Packet Type¶

    Bit 3 is 0 for a telemetry or reporting packet and 1 for a telecommand or requesting packet. The exact definitions belong to the project.18 The ECSS communications handbook observes that the split is not always obvious, for example on a link between two spacecraft.58

    Secondary Header Flag¶

    Bit 4 is 1 when a Packet Secondary Header is present and 0 when it is not. For a given APID and managed data path it must stay the same for a whole Mission Phase, so the receiver can rely on it. It is always 0 in an Idle Packet.18

    Naming in older documents

    ECSS-E-70-41A (PUS-A) and the Solar Orbiter packet ICD call this bit the Data Field Header Flag. The Solar Orbiter MIB names the bit-4 element DataFieldHeaderFlag and keeps the name "Secondary Header Flag" for a different bit inside the PUS data field header (see below).556575

    Application Process Identifier (APID)¶

    Bits 5 to 15 hold the APID, the naming mechanism for the managed data path. An APID may identify one sending or receiving application process inside a spacecraft. The only value with a fixed meaning is 2047 (0b11111111111), which marks Idle Packets.18 Issue 1 of the standard had reserved APIDs for carrying CFDP and LTP data units. Issue 2 removed them, so the link between an APID and a carried protocol is now mission-specific, and the SANA APID registry now reserves only the idle value.18 The registry policy recorded in Annex B is that 0 to 2046 are unmanaged and 2047 is reserved by the Blue Book.29

    ECSS adds rules of its own. In PUS-C every application process identifier is at most 2046. APID 2047 is reserved for idle packets and APID 0 for spacecraft time packets, and PUS-C refers to the SANA registry for other reserved values.42 PUS-A already reserved APID 0 for the time packet and the all-ones value for idle packets.56

    Sequence Flags¶

    Bits 16 and 17 describe segmentation, as shown in the table. Their use is optional. A Packet Service user may use them to show that a packet carries one segment of a larger body of application data.19

    Value Meaning
    0b00 Continuation segment
    0b01 First segment
    0b10 Last segment
    0b11 Unsegmented user data

    The Green Book gives the main use case: an application data unit too large for one packet, such as an image or a memory load, is cut into segments, the flags mark the first and last segment of the group so that the destination can rebuild it, and a segment number, if needed, can travel in the secondary header. It quotes the maximum Space Packet size as 65 536 octets, which is the largest Packet Data Field; the largest whole packet is 65 542 octets.9016 PUS-C always uses 0b11, so every PUS packet stands alone.46 PUS-A calls the TM bits "grouping flags", for use when several packets from one application process are sent as a group. All standard PUS-A packets are stand-alone.56

    Packet Sequence Count or Packet Name¶

    Bits 18 to 31 carry a Packet Sequence Count in every telemetry packet. In a telecommand packet they carry either a count or a Packet Name.19 The count numbers the packets that one user application (one APID) generates. Each APID has its own counter. The count runs continuously modulo 16 384 and must not be reset before 16 383 unless that cannot be avoided.19 The standard notes these consequences:19

    1. The count lets the receiver put back in order packets that transport shuffled, and lets it spot missing packets within each APID.
    2. After an unavoidable reset, the receiver can no longer tell whether a sequence is complete.
    3. A time code in the secondary header gives an unambiguous order over long periods. It has to advance at least once in each 16 384-packet cycle of the counter.
    4. A Packet Name identifies one packet among others in the same communications session and may be any 14-bit pattern.
    \[ \text{count}_{n+1} = (\text{count}_n + 1) \bmod 2^{14} \]

    Packet Data Length¶

    Bits 32 to 47 hold the length count C, one less than the number of octets in the Packet Data Field:20

    \[ C = N_{\text{data field}} - 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N_{\text{packet}} = C + 7 \]

    The smallest legal packet (C = 0) is 7 octets: 6 octets of header and 1 octet of data. The largest (C = 65 535) is 65 542 octets.1620 A receiver adds 7 to the count to find where the next packet starts. A TM or AOS packet extractor relies on exactly this. The position and meaning of the length field must be known to the data link service provider, which uses it with the frame's First Header Pointer to delimit packets.3736

    The Packet Data Field¶

    Packet Secondary Header¶

    If present, the secondary header follows the primary header with no gap. It is an integral number of octets. It is mandatory when there is no User Data Field and optional otherwise. Its contents are specified by the source end user and made known to destination users by management.21 It contains one of three options:21

    • a Time Code Field only;
    • an Ancillary Data Field only;
    • a Time Code Field followed by an Ancillary Data Field.

    The option chosen stays the same for a managed data path across all Mission Phases.21

    • Time Code Field: one of the CCSDS segmented or unsegmented binary time codes of CCSDS 301.0-B, for example CUC or CDS. The code chosen is fixed for the managed data path.     Inference

    CCSDS 133.0-B-2 lists PUS neither as a secondary header format nor in its SANA registry description. Treating the PUS secondary header as an "Ancillary Data Field" whose layout management makes known follows from clause 4.1.4.2. The documents themselves do not say so.

    User Data Field¶

    If present, the User Data Field follows the secondary header, or the primary header when there is no secondary header. It is mandatory when there is no secondary header and is an integral number of octets. In a normal packet it holds application data. In an Idle Packet it holds idle data, whose bit pattern the mission chooses.23

    Idle Packets¶

    An Idle Packet has APID 2047 and a Secondary Header Flag of 0, and its data field holds idle data, a fixed-length pattern that the project chooses.318 The TM, AOS and USLP data link protocols create Idle Packets when needed to keep their data transport processes synchronized.16 In TM and AOS, a frame that has to go out at its release time without enough user packets may be filled with an SPP Idle Packet or an Encapsulation Idle Packet. The shortest SPP Idle Packet is 7 octets. If the gap is smaller, the Idle Packet spills over into the next frame's data field.38 At the receiving end, Idle Packets are discarded.39

    The standard describes four abstract functions. They show logical relationships only and do not imply any hardware or software design.2425

    • Packet Assembly (sending end, one instance per Octet String entity). It builds the primary header, turns the Secondary Header Indicator into the Secondary Header Flag, and keeps the sequence counter.24
    • Packet Transfer (one per sending end system). It multiplexes packets from Packet Assembly and Packet Service users into a queue in an order set by management.     • Packet Reception (one per receiving end system). It receives packets and demultiplexes them by APID. It delivers them intact to Packet Service users, or passes them to Packet Extraction for Octet String users.25
    • Packet Extraction (one per Octet String entity). It removes the primary header, produces the Secondary Header Indicator, checks that the sequence count is continuous, and sets the optional Data Loss Indicator.25

    Carrying Space Packets over a space link¶

    How a packet sits inside Transfer Frames depends on the data link protocol:

    • TM and AOS: packets are concatenated into fixed-length frames until a frame is full. A packet that does not fit is split, and the rest continues in the next frame of the same Virtual Channel. The First Header Pointer marks the first packet header in each frame. Idle Packets fill frames when there is not enough user data.36
    • TC: when MAP channels are used, a packet too long for one frame is segmented, and its parts go in consecutive frames of the MAP channel with nothing interleaved. Without MAPs there is no segmentation, and each frame holds one or more complete packets. Receivers use each packet's length field to separate several packets in one frame.35

    Packets of different versions can share one Virtual Channel. The service provider multiplexes them into one packet stream and delivers each by its PVN.3439 The TM protocol's managed parameters for packet transfer are the valid PVNs, the maximum packet length, and whether incomplete packets must be delivered to the user.40 Issue 5 of the AOS protocol (October 2025) widens its First Header Pointer to 16 bits for frames of up to 65 536 octets; even then a maximum-length Space Packet of 65 542 octets does not fit in a single frame and is split across at least two (arithmetic by this wiki).9216

    Why missions cap the packet length

    While a long packet is being sent, other packets on the same Virtual Channel wait. PUS-C therefore notes that a mission may set a maximum telemetry packet length much shorter than the 65 542 octets the protocol allows.45 The ECSS communications handbook adds two more reasons. Losing one frame loses the whole packet it was part of. On slow telecommand links, an on-board application cannot use a long packet until all of it has arrived.58 Solar Orbiter caps TM packets at 4112 octets and TC packets at 248 octets (see below).

    SANA registries and security¶

    Issue 2 changed the existing SANA APID registry and added a registry of Space Packet Protocol Secondary Header Format Documents. Each entry records the format name, the submitting organization, a point of contact, the format source document and an optional URN. The working group's designated expert reviews entries. The registry does not change what goes on the wire. The Secondary Header Indicator of clause 3.4.2.3, which sets the Secondary Header Flag, remains the only sign that a secondary header is present, and which registered format applies is a managed parameter.29 The standard prefers that the format's source document describe the structure in a CCSDS notation such as SOIS Electronic Data Sheets or XTCE.29

    SPP provides no security. Authentication, confidentiality and integrity come from SDLS at the data link layer or from the Bundle Security Protocol at the network layer.28

    PUS-C secondary headers (ECSS-E-ST-70-41C, 2016)¶

    The telemetry packet secondary header starts with these fixed fields:48

    The time field holds the report's time tag, in a format declared when the time service is specified. A spare field may follow to pad the header to whole words.48 The telemetry user data field is the source data, an optional spare field for padding to the declared word size, and an optional 16-bit packet error control field. Whether TM checksums are used, and whether the CRC or the ISO 16-bit checksum, is decided when the standard is tailored for the mission.49

    The telecommand packet secondary header is:50

    The four acknowledgement bits ask for reports of successful acceptance (bit 3), start (bit 2), progress (bit 1) and completion (bit 0) of execution.50 The telecommand user data field is the application data, an optional spare field and a mandatory packet error control field, either CRC or ISO checksum as declared for the mission. On board, it is checked against the received packet.51

    PUS-A data field headers (ECSS-E-70-41A, 2003)¶

    PUS-A based its packets on the older CCSDS Packet Telemetry (102.0-B-5) and Telecommand (203.0-B-2) packet definitions and called the secondary header the data field header.5556

    The TM data field header is a spare bit, a 3-bit PUS version (1), 4 spare bits, the service type and subtype, an optional packet sub-counter, an optional destination ID, the time (CUC or CDS, set by mission constants) and spare bits.56 The TC packet error control is mandatory. The TM packet error control is optional and fixed per application process. PUS-A reserves service types 0 to 127 for the standard and 128 to 255 for missions.5556 It requires a separate sequence count for each telecommand source and APID, and copies the TC packet's sequence control into the acknowledgement report.55

    PUS-C Rev.1 draft (DIR1, 2024)¶

    The December 2024 draft (DIR1) separates ground and on-board identifiers. Any application process identifier may go up to 65 535, while an on-board one stays at or below 2046 to fit the 11-bit APID. Ground application process identifiers run from 2048 to 65 535, and no identifier may name both a ground and an on-board process. The draft also references CCSDS 133.0-B-2 for the idle APID.57 See PUS-A vs PUS-C.

    Request IDs, CPDU packets and the PUS CRC¶

    Two PUS details refer straight back to the primary header:

    • A ST[01] request verification report names the request it verifies by its request ID, which is a copy of the telecommand's packet ID and packet sequence control fields (PVN, type, secondary header flag, APID, sequence flags, count). The request ID alone is not unique, because the request's source is in the report's destination ID.52
    • A CPDU command packet is a bare Space Packet. Its secondary header flag is 0, its APID is the CPDU's, its sequence flags are 0b11 and its count or name is 0. It has no secondary header, and its packet error control is the same 16-bit CRC used in the telecommand frame's error control field.53 See ST[02] device access.

    The PUS CRC is CRC-16-CCITT with generator \(x^{16}+x^{12}+x^5+1\). The register starts at all ones and covers the whole packet except the error control field. The standard gives test vectors such as 00 00 giving 1D 0F and AB CD EF 01 giving 04 A2.54

    For the categories 1, 7, 9 and 12, the MIB mostly follows the ICD's rules (see the counts in the table). Some categories that the ICD reserves for one service hold packets of other services. The clearest case is service 5 (events), which the ICD reserves for PCAT 7.     The TC packet header as the MIB defines it¶

    SCOS-2000 builds the start of each encoded telecommand from a packet header definition (tcp, pcpc, pcdf). Each element is fixed (F) or filled from the command record (A APID, T type, S subtype, K acknowledgement flags), or the command subsystem sets it (P, for example the length and sequence counter).59 Solar Orbiter's nominal header NOM_TC (used by 5318 commands) is 80 bits. It is the 48-bit CCSDS primary header followed by a 32-bit PUS-A data field header:75

    The header HPC1_TC (125 commands, all on APID 44, flagged high priority with CCF_MAPID 0) is only the 48-bit primary header, with the DataFieldHeaderFlag set to 0. This is the CPDU "HPC-1" command that goes straight to the Command Pulse Distribution Unit on MAP 0.7567 NO_HDR ("TCP Header for CF/CS") is used by 5 commands whose CCF_CTYPE is R, a control segment, or F, a control frame (BC frames such as FARM Unlock and Set V(R)). For such commands the import ICD says that the PCDF and CDF entries define the frame data field instead of a packet header and application data field.61 In the Solar Orbiter MIB the NO_HDR header has no pcdf rows, so only the cdf rows describe the frame data field. UNLOCK, for example, is a single fixed octet of value 0.75 NOS2KHDR (6 commands, CCF_CTYPE N) carries NIS throw events on APID 1964. The ICD says that the NIS executes these itself and does not forward them to the ground station equipment.7561

    These features match the ICD. Version 0b000, type 1, a data field header on every command except CPDU commands, sequence flags 0b11, a CCSDS secondary header flag of 0 inside the data field header, and a PUS version of 1.6566 Three details deserve a note:

    • Acknowledgement flags. The ICD allows only the acceptance and completion bits on Solar Orbiter and requires the start and progress bits to be zero.66 In the MIB, CCF_ACK = 9 (0b1001, acceptance plus completion) for 5225 of the 5318 NOM_TC commands, 1 for 78 and 0 for 15.75
    • Sequence count. The ICD says the count is kept per APID and Source ID, and that on-board applications do not check it.65 The SCOS-2000 import ICD says the multiplexer keeps the source sequence counter for each APID.59
    • Source ID. Ground sources have reserved values: mission timeline 110, TC sequences 111, recovery actions 112, back-up timeline 113, direct commands 120, and spares 121 and 122. On-board sources are 15 (OBCP), 14 (system control) and 11 (AOCS).74 In the April 2020 MIB the Source ID was a fixed area with value 0. In the 2025 release it is a header parameter (DFH_SRC) with element type O.7675 The separate SWA instrument database fixes it at 0x78, which is 120, "direct commands".77

    Inference

    The element type O does not appear in the PCDF_TYPE codes of the SCOS-2000 import ICD issues 5.1, 6.7, 6.9 and 7.0 in the library (F, A, T, S, K, P). Its exact meaning is not stated in the available sources. Changing the Source ID from a fixed value to a parameter is consistent with letting the ground system set different source IDs (for example 120 for direct commands and 110 for the mission timeline).5974

    Lengths, time packets and idle packets¶

    • TC. The packet data field is at most 242 octets: 4 octets of data field header, 236 of application data and 2 of packet error control. A complete TC packet is therefore at most 248 octets.65 The PEC is the CRC-16 with all-ones preset described above, with the same test vectors.72 SCOS-2000's default maximum command size (CMDP_MAX_BLOCK_SIZE) is the same 248 octets.60
    • TM. A packet is at most 4112 octets. The data field is at most 4106 octets: 10 octets of data field header and 4096 of source data. Packets longer than the 1099-octet TM frame data field are split across frames.68 The 10-octet data field header (shown below) carries a PUS version of 1, an 8-bit Destination ID and a CUC time with 4 octets of coarse and 2 of fine time.69 In the MIB, 11 138 of the 11 150 packet definitions have PID_DFHSIZE = 16, which is 6 octets of primary header plus 10 of data field header, the offset at which SCOS-2000 starts processing variable packets.7562
    • Sequence count and grouping. A TM source sequence count is kept per APID and Destination ID. It starts at zero at power-on and wraps from 214−1 to 0. The ground raises an alarm on every gap. The grouping flags mark sets of self-contained packets that together answer one command.68
    • Time packet. APID 0 has no data field header. Its 8-octet data field (C = 7) holds a 1-octet S-field (rate), the P-field 0b00101110 (CUC with 4 coarse and 2 fine octets) and a 6-octet T-field.70 In the MIB it is SPID 9999, with PID_DFHSIZE = 6 and TPCF_SIZE = 14.75
    • Idle packet. APID all ones, version 0, type 0, data field header flag 0, random filler data.71 The MIB defines it as SPID 66986 on APID 2047.75
    • No TM packet error control. The ICD's TM source packet figure shows the packet data field as the 80-bit data field header plus source data only, while its TC packet figure ends with a 16-bit packet error control field. The 4106-octet maximum is likewise all header and source data, and Annex 6 describes the packet error control as the two trailing octets of a TC packet.686572 The MIB agrees. PID_CHECK, which tells SCOS-2000 whether to check a packet error control field, is 0 for every definition except six ground-equipment records (the three APID-1 frame records described below and three MATIS packets on APID 1966).7564

    The TM data field header¶

    The fields are 1 spare bit, the 3-bit PUS version (0b001), 4 spare bits, the 8-bit service type and subtype, an 8-bit Destination ID and a 48-bit CUC time, 80 bits in all. Compared with PUS-A, the optional packet sub-counter is not used.6956 The Destination ID of a report that answers a telecommand is a copy of the command's Source ID, except in packet categories 2, 3, 4, 8 and 9, which always carry 0 (ground). Telemetry produced for a process in another software item carries that process's ID, and all other telemetry carries 0.74

    Inference

    The MIB packet size of the TM(1,1) report (SPID 14181, TPCF_SIZE = 20) is exactly 6 + 10 + 4 octets, with its two 16-bit source parameters at octets 16 and 18 and no room for a checksum. This supports the reading above.75 TPCF_SIZE is only informative, and SCOS-2000 does not use it. The ICD recommends including the 64- or 76-byte SCOS-2000 packet header in it, which the Solar Orbiter values (14 and 20) evidently do not, so they appear to count the source packet alone.63

    APIDs outside the 11-bit range¶

    The MIB also defines six packets on APIDs 2048 to 2065, named "LMS_..." low and high frequency. They cannot appear in a Space Packet header. The SCOS-2000 import ICD keeps APIDs 0 to 2047 for packets from the spacecraft and allows 2048 to 65 535 for packets made by SCOS-2000 applications or other control domains such as SCOE equipment.7562 Three more definitions on APID 1 with PID_DFHSIZE = 0 describe "good", "bad" and "idle complete transfer frames".

    Three similar definitions with PID_DFHSIZE = 6 on APID 1966 (process ID 122, in the EGSE range) describe "good", "bad" and "idle mini-transfer frames".7573

    Inference

    Those APID-1 and APID-1966 definitions record frame quality made on the ground and are not packets sent by the spacecraft. The MIB descriptions suggest this, but no source in the library documents it.

    Edition history¶

    Edition Date Main points
    CCSDS 133.0-B-1 September 2003 First issue, now superseded.1 It replaced three earlier packet definitions that shared the same PVN: the Source Packet of Packet Telemetry, the TC Packet of Packet Telecommand and the Version-1 CCSDS Packet of AOS.58
    CCSDS 133.0-B-2 June 2020 Removes networking terminology and implied function, and brings the text up to date with modern practice.1 The reserved CFDP and LTP APIDs are dropped, and a SANA registry of secondary header formats is added.1829
    EC 1 October 2020 Corrects figure 2-1: adds a missing arrowhead and makes the choice between EPP and SPP exclusive.1
    EC 2 September 2024 Fixes duplicated table-of-contents entries and moves to A4 pages.1

    The networking language that Issue 2 removed can still be seen in ECSS-E-HB-50A (2008), which describes Issue 1. That text uses a Logical Data Path named by a Path ID, made of the APID plus an optional APID Qualifier that is not in the packet. Over a space link the qualifier was the link's Master Channel Identifier. It also places SPP in the Network Layer and calls its main function "routing".58 Issue 2 replaces this with the managed data path and states that SPP has no routing function.65 PUS-C (2016) still cites Issue 1. The 2024 Rev.1 draft cites Issue 2 in its normative references and in the APID notes, although its introduction still names CCSDS 133.0-B-1.4457 The companion Green Book 130.3-G-1 (April 2023) is written against Issue 2 and its EPP counterpart 133.1-B-3, and its reference list cites the data link protocols of 2020 and 2021, AOS still at Issue 4.80
