    SCOS-2000: ESA's mission control system¶

    SCOS-2000 (often shortened to S2K) is the generic mission control system (MCS) developed by ESA's European Space Operations Centre (ESOC) in Darmstadt. It receives, processes, displays and archives spacecraft telemetry, and it prepares, releases and verifies telecommands. For more than two decades it has been the kernel on which ESOC builds the dedicated control system of each mission,1681 and it has also served as the core of instrument and spacecraft checkout systems.1356 A mission does not program SCOS-2000. It configures it, mainly with a database that describes its telemetry, telecommands and displays: the Mission Information Base (MIB).171

    This page tells the history of SCOS-2000 and puts it in context: its ancestors, its releases, the missions and organisations that used it, its licensing and user community, its independent quality certification, the "mission family" strategy that made it cheaper, ESOC's own comparison of its four control-system generations, and the planned replacement by the European Ground Systems Common Core (EGS-CC). The companion pages explain the internals:

    • SCOS-2000 architecture: servers, client applications, the telemetry and telecommand chains, archives, events, external interfaces and configuration.
    • SCOS-2000 as EGSE and central checkout: the EGSE variant, the TOPE test language and the Herschel/Planck checkout systems.
    • Operations preparation: the databases, procedures and tools that prepare a SCOS-2000 mission, with the INTEGRAL and CHEOPS practice.
    • Ancillary systems, MICONYS and the move to EGS-CC, and the pages on EDDS, TDRS, archives, NCTRS and NIS and time correlation.

    The packet standards that SCOS-2000 implements have their own history, from the ESA PSS standards to CCSDS and ECSS; it is told on From ESA PSS to CCSDS and ECSS.

    What a mission control system does¶

    ESA's Bulletin describes the MCS as the software that lets the operators on the ground interact with a satellite. It receives, interprets, analyses and archives the telemetry, and it generates, verifies and uplinks the commands that control the satellite's operations. Through it the Flight Control Team assesses the health of the satellite and commands it to meet the mission goals. The MCS is critical, so it goes through comprehensive verification and validation, and so does the operational data that configures it, such as the database of the spacecraft's telemetry and command data. An MCS usually also supports mission planning, data analysis and distribution, database management and on-board software management.7

    In 2003 ESA listed the functions that SCOS-2000 covers: telemetry processing, telecommanding, an online database, an online archive, general services and utilities, an operations language (OL), roles and privileges, events and actions, and on-board software maintenance (OBSM).1 A mission configures these functions to its own needs through the database (telemetry, telecommand and display characteristics) and through the system configuration files.1 The architecture page describes how these functions are split into server processes and client applications.

    Lineage: from MSSS to SCOS-2000¶

    ESOC has built its mission control systems on reusable infrastructure since the 1970s. Its stated objective is reusable infrastructure for any class of orbit and mission, with the same system reused wherever possible for the MCS, the EGSE and station control.12 In 1974 ESOC decided to develop a reusable MCS infrastructure for the missions it operated. This started a product line whose first member, the Multi Mission Support System (MSSS), entered service in 1977.25 The Bulletin article of 2007 calls MSSS the "Multi-Satellite Support System", developed in the 1970s and used for over 20 years. It dates the first generation of the Spacecraft Control and Operations System (SCOS) to the mid-1980s and its replacement by SCOS-2000 to the late 1990s.5 In 2019 ESOC counted "more than 40 years" of this infrastructure-based approach and four generations: MSSS, SCOS 1, SCOS II (renamed SCOS-2000) and the EGS-CC-based M4C, compared in Four generations compared below.102

    SCOS-II: the new generation (1994)¶

    The SCOS-II paper, written by ESOC's Mission Operations Department, explains why ESOC replaced SCOS-I. Its reference list, and that of the OL paper below, place both papers in the SpaceOps 94 proceedings.3240 The concepts of MSSS and SCOS-I came from the mission control needs of the 1970s, when spacecraft were hard-wired. Spacecraft such as EURECA and ERS-1 carried much more on-board software and autonomy, and the old systems reached their limits.25 The new system had to reduce mission adaptation and maintenance costs, make mission preparation, execution and evaluation more efficient, increase operational quality and reliability, last at least 10 years, and serve missions of any type, size and complexity.25 It also had to be compatible with the CCSDS and ESA packet standards and with the guidelines of ESA's Committee for Operations and EGSE Standards (COES).25

    The design choices made for SCOS-II still shape SCOS-2000:

    • Client-server distribution. A network of Unix workstations, with a dedicated workstation for each operational user and central servers for system-level services such as the ground station interface. One SCOS-II instance controls several satellites of the same family rather than several unrelated missions.26
    • Scalability and roles. The same system can be the prime MCS, a "mini-backup" at a TT&C station, or (because it follows COES) a checkout system. For a simple mission it can be descoped to a single Sun workstation or even a PC.27
    • Database-driven configuration. Not only TM/TC processing but display layouts, default behaviours and user privilege sets come from the mission database.27
    • Ambitious performance goals. Concurrent real-time rates of 2 Mbps telemetry and 4 kbps telecommand, display updates faster than 10 per second, retrievals within 5 s for data a few weeks old and 30 s for data several years old, with up to 50 workstations in use.27
    • Parameter validity. Each parameter's validity combines power status, the quality of the data unit that carried it, the data route, the age of the value, stability after commanding, and user-defined criteria. Invalidity propagates, for example to synthetic parameters that use an invalid input.29
    • Three kinds of checks. Operational status checks, operational constraint checks (rules that must never be violated) and behaviour checks against the predicted on-board state.29
    • Uniform "activities". A procedure, a command sequence and a single command are all activities with pre-requisites, execution monitoring and verification. Pre-execution validation combines feasibility, safety and dynamic checks. Activities are released by a scheduler, a manual stack or an event-driven commander.30
    • Online history. Replay "as live" or as "video replay", data access strategies, data analysis and report generation were to become part of the MCS itself.31

    SCOS-II also planned a mission model of "system elements" with operational modes and roles, a library of reusable elements and a prediction function.28 Release 1 was due as a preliminary delivery in November 1994, with releases 2 (1995–6) and 3 (1996–7) adding modelling, mission planning and data distribution. The first client missions were to be Huygens, Artemis and Envisat, launched in 1997–1998.32

    Inference

    Several SCOS-II ambitions, such as the system-element mission model and the event-driven commander, do not appear as such in SCOS-2000's database interface (the MIB tables describe parameters, packets, commands and displays, not a model of system elements). The idea of operating through a model of the space system returns with the Monitoring and Control Model of EGS-CC.88 This reading is the wiki's own; the sources do not draw the connection.

    The SCOS-II Operations Language (OL)¶

    A companion SpaceOps 94 paper by ESOC and Computer Resources International (CRI) introduced the SCOS-II Operations Language (OL).33 Its motivation was the slow turnaround of derived parameters in earlier systems. In MSSS an operations engineer wrote an algorithm on paper and a software engineer coded it. In SCOS-I the engineer wrote FORTRAN with a few extra constructs, which was compiled and linked into the control system, so an error only showed up as a run-time crash. Neither system supported version and configuration control of these algorithms.34 The OL let operations engineers edit, test, validate and install expressions and procedures themselves, without software support engineers.33

    The paper divides mission knowledge into four kinds: declarative (for example calibration curves, entered through forms), expressive (derived parameters, command validation conditions, written in the OL), procedural (operations procedures, also in the OL) and special knowledge that still needs software extensions.35 The implementation has three layers: an interaction layer (the user interface), a logical layer centred on the OL, and a physical layer with the control system's services. An explicit object-oriented data model is the "contract" between the logical and physical layers.3536

They noted that OL scripts are hard to port between missions whose data models differ.40 SCOS-2000 kept the OL for synthetic parameters: the MIB delivers one OL expression file per dynamic synthetic parameter, and the Solar Orbiter MIB of September 2025 contains 1,582 of them (see the architecture page and synthetic parameters and the OL language).6496

    From SCOS-2 to SCOS-2000¶

    An ESOC presentation of April 2000 explains the name. The system was called "SCOS-2" up to release R1.4 (until 1998) and "SCOS-2000" from release R2.0 (from 1999). SCOS-2000 is an evolution of SCOS-2 based on operational experience. Its differences are a new telecommand chain, architectural consolidation, a user interface fully based on ILOG, systematic use of the ObjectStore object database, and CORBA for better distribution and for new external interfaces.42 ESA's Bulletin of November 2001 gives the same history from another angle. Work on SCOS started in about 1992, the first ESA missions were supported in 1997, and the telecommanding chain was re-engineered in 1998–2000. The total investment, including ESA's internal effort, was about 15 MEuro.53

    Release 5 and later

    The available sources do not give the release date of R5.0, and no ESOC document in the library describes R5 as a whole. What the Database Import ICD shows is that R5.0 stored telemetry packets "in a database" (so there was no longer any gain in letting several packets share one SPID), moved command sequences and displays out of the run-time database into the FARC, and added SLE and NIS throw-event commands.6061 The year of R6.0 is also not stated in the available sources; the earliest dated mention found is the EDDS release note of February 2018, which lists SCOS 6.0.1 as a tested dependency.66

    Licensing: ESA software as a European product¶

    Operational software and its licences¶

    ESA's rules normally give the intellectual property rights (IPR) of software developed under ESA contract to the contractor, with ESA free to use it. A category called operational software is an exception. ESA may claim full IPR for it, which guarantees inspection of the source code, the possibility to move the software between contractors (for example from development to maintenance), and ESA's independence from the original author. For software that ESA owns, ESA may grant a non-exclusive licence to a body in a Member State, for peaceful purposes in space research and technology, normally free of charge.52 ESA had tried a different approach with MSSS, letting licensees gain IPR on changed code, and found that it led to "forking" and little feedback to the original product.52

    In the absence of detailed ESA rules for licensing agreements, ESOC used three types of licence for its software in practice:55

    Licence type Purpose Source code
    Run-time licence Temporary use and trial No right to change or inspect the source
    Development licence Upgrades of operational software for ESOC's own use, under ESA contract Work on the source code allowed
    Full licence Developments based on the software, for example a product Changes allowed

    The licence offer made to the Herschel/Planck instrument teams in April 2000 used different categories: documentation only (with access to an FTP server), a trial version for a limited time, the run-time system, or the source code. Under ESA's convention for operational software, any Member State body could apply free of charge. The licence was non-exclusive, ESA kept ownership (it could not be sold on), and the licensee had to buy the hardware and any commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) licences itself.4946

    In 2003 ESOC presented the arrangement as close to open source, but restricted to Europe. ESA acted as the "gatekeeper" of the software and kept the IPR. Companies could use SCOS-2000 in any offer, including outside Europe, and were encouraged to hand improvements back for inclusion in the product. Business was to be made with services, not with the software itself.14 The Bulletin of 2001 discussed an "open-source-type" model in which licensees return improvements to the SCOS-2000 kernel, while industry stays free to sell add-on products that use the defined interfaces.55 The Planck LFI paper of 2010 describes SCOS-2000 as available "under an open-source licensing scheme".71

    ESA Software Community Licence

    A 2019 ESOC paper states that the EGS-CC preparation environment OPEN and its mission and ground-station extensions (OPEN-M and OPEN-S) would be made available to the EGS-CC community under the ESA Software Community License.94 Whether SCOS-2000 itself, or the MICONYS suite around it, is distributed under that licence is not stated in the available sources.

    Community¶

    ESOC treated product promotion as a commitment with real costs. It set up a user organisation, gave access to documentation through an FTP server, and started the help desk. In November 2001 a web site with documentation, FAQs, user registration, a problem-report database, release plans and an online demonstration was under construction, due before the end of that year.56 Nearly 100 people from outside ESA attended the first SCOS-2000 Users Workshop in April 2001. The workshop presented the product and its evolution, proposed a SCOS-2000 User Community, and heard users' own experience reports. The proposed open-source approach was received positively.56

    ESOC and the users were to be partners. ESOC is the custodian of the product: it maintains it, fixes faults, follows platform changes, integrates features developed by user missions and performs the research that keeps it up to date. Users propose requirements from operational experience and contribute code.56 At the April 2000 meeting with the Herschel/Planck instrument teams, ESOC stated that it guards the integrity of the product line ("one product only"). Changes are classified as infrastructure enhancements (ESA funded) or project-specific (customer funded), and project-specific changes are implemented as add-ons to the kernel.50

    Quality: the SPEC certification¶

    To support the promotion of SCOS-2000, ESA started an evaluation and certification of the product by an independent organisation.572 The method, SPEC (Software Product Evaluation and Certification), was developed under ESA contract and based on several international standards; Bulletin 108 names ISO 9126 among them. SCOS-2000 was the largest product evaluated with SPEC up to 2003.572

    SPEC's quality model breaks goal properties into properties, measures each property with metrics, and computes each metric with an evaluation method.23 It includes properties of the development process as well as of the product, and covers space-specific practices from ECSS-E-40 and ECSS-Q-80:3

    Product-related goal properties Process-related goal properties
    Functionality, Reliability, Maintainability, Re-usability, Operability, Documentation Quality Suitability for Safety, Software Development Effectiveness, System Engineering Effectiveness

    The evaluation had two phases: a baseline evaluation of SCOS-2000 version 2.1e, then a delta evaluation and certification of an improved version. A Software Criticality Analysis based on a software FMECA preceded the first phase but was not part of SPEC.4 Of the 85 SPEC metrics, 10 were not applicable and 3 were replaced by similar metrics for lack of a tool or method. Of the remaining 72, 5 missed their target value. SCOS-2000 met most targets but needed improvement before it could be certified as Class-B (mission critical) software. The recommendations asked for homogeneous documentation quality, better traceability from requirements to design, code and tests, architectural changes to reduce the complexity of some modules, and better coding standards.4 New coding standards covering C++ and Java and model-driven architecture were planned.4

    The evaluator and certifier was TÜV InterTraffic. Critical Software (Portugal) performed the criticality analysis, ESOC's Ground Segment Engineering Department requested the certification, and ESTEC's Software Product Assurance Section supervised the method.4 Bulletin 108 had named TÜV Cologne as the certifier two years earlier.57

    Cutting costs: products, families and the delta approach¶

    SCOS-2000 as a product¶

    SCOS-2000 had four strategic aims: easy configuration and customisation, functional richness (less mission-specific code), scalability (add workstations as needed) and vendor independence (no forced ports when platforms or COTS become obsolete). ESA reported in 2001 that all four were achieved and that the cost of ESOC control systems fell by a factor of two to three.53 Vendor independence came with the "evolution version": Linux instead of Solaris, and open-source POST++ and omniORB instead of ObjectStore and Orbix. ILOG Views remained for the user interface.5421 A study found a Windows port feasible, but ESA had not decided to proceed.57

    ESOC listed the characteristics of a good generic MCS: generic, customisable, open with well-defined interfaces, well documented, easy to install, portable across popular platforms, minimally dependent on COTS, supported long-term, with expertise available on a competitive market, and free of IPR obstacles to distribution.16 The 2001 Bulletin saw the commercial competition in US suppliers such as Integral Systems, Storm and Braxton Technologies, whose products were closed, carried licence fees and were expensive to customise for new mission classes.55

    Mission families and the delta approach¶

    ESOC then reused software at a second level. A mission family is a set of missions with a high degree of commonality in platform or operational profile. ESOC's families were interplanetary, Earth observation, observatory and navigation missions.8 In the delta approach, software is organised in three layers, each defined only by its differences from the layer below:977

    Requirements documents become "delta" documents that list only mission-specific requirements. Design keeps interfaces unchanged where possible, testing focuses on mission features, and the SCOS-2000 kernel is maintained separately.977 A configuration control board decides when requirements move down a layer. A successful family kernel eventually shrinks to nothing, as its features migrate into SCOS-2000.911

    The two families used the approach differently. For Rosetta, Mars Express and Venus Express a two-layer approach gave a single system that served all three missions by reconfiguration.10 For CryoSat, GOCE and Aeolus a three-layer approach with an Earth Observation Mission Kernel absorbed the differences in spacecraft platform.10 CryoSat was the first mission defined as a delta against SCOS-2000, GOCE as a delta against CryoSat, and Aeolus as a delta against the Earth Explorer kernel.78 The requirement counts at definition phase show the effect:79

    EGOS¶

    ESOC grouped its reusable ground software under the name EGOS (ESA Ground Operations Software system), a family of products following the SCOS-2000 approach.23 The 2003 vision listed, among others, ground systems test and validation, a multi-mission configuration control manager, ground systems monitoring and control, NCTRS/SLE, the SIMSAT simulator infrastructure, generic file transfer and data disposition systems, operations preparation, data analysis, automated procedure execution, an archive, EGSE, mission planning, a TM/TC baseband system and flight dynamics.24 By 2007 the Bulletin called SCOS-2000 and SIMSAT the cornerstones of EGOS.5 By 2014 most EGOS user interfaces, SCOS-2000's included, were built on one Java and Eclipse-based framework, the EGOS User Desktop, described with the other products on ancillary systems.128

    Four generations compared (2019)¶

    At the ICALEPCS conference of 2019 (New York), M. Pecchioli of ESOC gave a technical comparison of the mission control infrastructure generations developed at ESOC. The paper starts from an observation: the basic needs of mission operators have not radically changed since the first spacecraft operations, but the implementation of the infrastructure has. Five factors drove that evolution: spacecraft orbits (from long or continuous visibility with short delays to short passes in polar orbits or long delays in deep space), space asset design (the TM/TC protocol, the on-board monitoring and control services, data volumes and rates, and on-board autonomy), operations concepts (from manual commanding to procedures, ground automation and on-board autonomy, to cut the cost of long routine phases), software technologies and IT infrastructure.99

    Why an infrastructure at all¶

    Most space organisations, the paper notes, develop each MCS on a fully mission-specific life cycle. ESA instead built its operational ground segment on infrastructure that is specific to a domain but not to a mission, for strategic and programmatic rather than technical reasons: costs shared across missions are stable, predictable and low; a "nearly ready" solution shortens the preparation of a new mission; a proven common infrastructure reduces risk; and staff who move between missions need less familiarisation.100 These benefits only materialise with a careful design, and the paper lists the drivers that a mission-specific system would weigh less:101

    Design driver What the paper means by it
    Support of heterogeneous space systems Standard TM/TC protocols reduced the number of protocols to support, and PUS standardised access to on-board functions, so that common ground solutions became possible
    Support of multiple mission types The orbit sets the type: long passes and short delays (geostationary), short passes and short delays (polar Earth observation), long passes and long delays (deep space)
    Standardised interfaces Generic file-based and programmatic interfaces to preparation, planning, execution and evaluation systems; international (CCSDS) standards between facilities of different organisations, such as control centres and ground stations
    Modular architecture Hierarchical decomposition with separation of concerns, so that generic and mission-specific parts can be developed in parallel
    Generic and extensible functionality Extensibility means integrating application-specific features without changing the infrastructure source code
    Capability to tailor A data-driven design: the TM/TC characteristics are defined in a database that converts human-readable definitions into data units
    Functional richness More functions than the specified needs, for safe and "comfortable" operations
    Layered design Strict separation of reusable parts from mission extensions, through interfaces and services; ideally no infrastructure change is needed to build a full MCS
    Operations abstraction Operators concentrate on "what" has to be done, not on low-level details
    Support of automation Automation at all levels, from deployment to the execution of planned operations, to control routine costs
    High performance Storage, distribution and processing rates, data volumes and parallel users, without dependence on expensive hardware
    Longevity and long-term maintainability Decades of use without explosive costs, with the expertise continuously created and sustained
    Portability and technology isolation Business logic isolated from the reused off-the-shelf technologies
    Scalability From large constellations to small low-cost satellites, on scalable computing and storage

    Towards EGS-CC¶

    By the 2010s ESOC expected the SCOS-2000 generation to face significant obsolescence, with rising maintenance and evolution costs.8692 An industrial view from 2010 is blunt. Terma, which had delivered several SCOS-2000-based checkout systems, found any system based on SCOS-2000 complex to develop, configure and use. Its telecommand chain handled about 2 packets per second with checking and 20 without, and keeping it on modern platforms was becoming more expensive, not less.73

    ESA's answer is the European Ground Systems Common Core (EGS-CC), a European initiative for a common monitoring and control kernel. ESOC's adoption project is called EGOS-CC.8689 ESOC's papers stress what changes. SCOS-2000 assumes a spacecraft that exchanges PUS TM/TC packets, while EGS-CC abstracts any controlled system, including ground stations.87 Teams familiar with the "SCOS-2000 MIB ICD" must learn the EGS-CC Conceptual Data Model, and the technology moves away from C++, CORBA and SQL.88 EGS-CC is to be used as a third-party "black box" product, unlike SCOS-2000, which missions were free to modify.88

    SCOS-2000 and the complementary systems around it form MICONYS (MIssion COntrol sYStem), ESOC's suite of generic mission control products. Their EGS-CC-based successor is called MICONYS-CC in an undated ESOC paper (it plans development to start in the second quarter of 2017) and M4C (Mission Control Core based on Common Core) in a 2019 paper, with a functional scope "very similar" to SCOS-2000.818993 ESOC's ICALEPCS paper of the same year says M4C provides features equivalent to "SCOS-2000 and its complementary systems" and compares it with its predecessors (see above).102 The MIB remains central during the transition. ESOC sorts missions into three categories: missions that keep a SCOS-2000 MIB and its tools (DABYS, MOIS, ProToS, MATIS, FOPPER) and use a MIB to EGS-CC converter (Swarm); missions that convert all tailoring data once to the EGS-CC formats (expected for Ariel); and missions that receive EGS-CC tailoring data directly from industry.85 See ancillary systems, MICONYS and EGS-CC and mission tailoring of the MIB.
