    SCOS-2000 as EGSE and central checkout¶

    Before a spacecraft flies, it is tested on the ground. Its units, instruments and finally the complete satellite are connected to electrical ground support equipment (EGSE): the electrical and electronic equipment that supports assembly, integration, functional and environmental testing, ground station compatibility tests and launch operations.1 The heart of a system-level EGSE is the Central Checkout System (CCS), from which tests are prepared, run and archived. Around it sit Specific Check Out Equipment (SCOE) units, each dedicated to one subsystem or interface.122

    ESA designed SCOS-2000 to serve both mission control and checkout. Its stated objective is to reuse the same system for the MCS, the EGSE and station control wherever possible.16 This page describes how that worked in practice: the PROBA heritage, the "EGSE and Mission Control System" (EMCS) that ESA delivered to the Herschel/Planck instrument teams, the TOPE test language, the Herschel/Planck instrument and central checkout systems, commercial products that keep SCOS-2000 compatibility, translators between databases, and the migration of TOPE procedures to EGS-CC. The internals of the kernel are on SCOS-2000 architecture; the Herschel/Planck interface documents behind the test systems (router, PIPE, CCS external interfaces) are read field by field on Herschel/Planck interface documents.

    Why one system for test and operations¶

    Several ESA policies pushed checkout and operations towards common tools. In April 2000 the FIRST/Planck (later Herschel/Planck) project summarised ESA's science directorate policy: EGSE is procured by industry as part of the spacecraft contract, the agency delivers as little as possible to industry, and commonality between the flight control system and the checkout system is to be pursued within the project's constraints. The same goal applied to Rosetta and Mars Express.29 The Herschel/Planck EGSE specification lists the expected benefits: less duplicated effort, lower risk and cost, and more synergy between AIV and flight operations. It names the areas where commonality is pursued: the Mission Information Base, user interfaces, data archiving and distribution, on-board software management and maintenance, and the user language for test procedures and in-orbit operations.7

    ESA's Bulletin of 2007 confirms the result: common tools for spacecraft checkout and for operations preparation gave a seamless transition from manufacture to operations, which Herschel and Planck exploited.17 The modifications that made SCOS-2000 usable for spacecraft and instrument checkout were pioneered for the PROBA technology satellite and then merged into the evolution version of SCOS-2000.18

    The PROBA heritage¶

    PROBA (Project for On-Board Autonomy) was a small technology satellite with a 32-bit processor, an Earth-observation imager and a test bed for automatic operations, including on-board mission planning. Its ground station was a 2 m antenna at Redu, independent of the ESA network.30 For PROBA, ESA built a common EGSE and mission control system on SCOS-2 (release R1.4), and planned to move it to SCOS-2000 R2.2 by the end of 2000.3423 In April 2000 ESOC offered the reusable PROBA elements plus two additional database and procedure tools:202425

    Element Description
    SCOS-2000 The kernel
    EGSE router TM reception, TC transmission and the SCOS gateway
    Procedure automation tool Based on the free Tcl/Tk language; used for complex procedures in AIV and in PROBA operations
    Database editors Based on ORACLE, used for both spacecraft and SCOE data (the SCOE equipment was addressed like a spacecraft subsystem)
    Integral database editor (additional) Based on MS-Access, used for INTEGRAL
    Winfops/MOIS (additional) Based on MS-Access, for procedure preparation and simple (tabular) automation

    The meeting minutes add that messages to the SCOEs were routed by APID, using APIDs outside the spacecraft range, and that the PROBA procedure environment read the ORACLE database directly at compile time.30 On a Windows NT PC, the router sustained 1 Mbps of telemetry (input through a DMA parallel card) and 4 kbps of telecommands (through an RS-422 card) at less than 15 % CPU load. Distributing the data cost about 2 % CPU per virtual channel.21 Full ground-space commanding needed a commercial product, the "TC Encoder Shell" from De Lande Long, priced at about 10 kEUR per PC and needed only when the CCSDS COP-1 protocol was used.2226

    The EMCS: EGSE and Mission Control System¶

    The document "EGSE based on SCOS 2000: the EGSE & Mission Control System", a software user manual written at ESTEC, describes the EGSE version of SCOS-2000 that ESA delivered to the Herschel/Planck instrument developers. Issue 4.0 of March 2004 covers the "SCOS EGSE Release 2.3e P5", installed on Linux SuSE 7.3. The manual complements the generic SCOS-2000 documentation with installation and configuration procedures, the TOPE language reference and the EGSE message formats.3332 Earlier issues followed SCOS-2000 R2.1.1 (2001) and R2.3e with ESOC patches (2003). Issue 3.0 introduced a new language grammar to ease the transfer of procedures to the Herschel/Planck CCS.32

    The Space System Model and system element identifiers¶

    The Space System Model (SSM) is an information system holding the mission information that the EMCS and its users need. The concept comes from ECSS-E-00 and was to be specified in ECSS-E-70-31 (see ECSS-E-ST-70-31C).35 The space system is a hierarchy of system elements (SE): the spacecraft with its data handling system, on-board software and application processes, and the ground segment with the EMCS, SCOS and the SCOEs. Elements that take part in monitoring and control get a 16-bit SE identifier. For on-board application processes, the SE identifier is the APID; the 11-bit APID field of CCSDS packets reserves 0x0000 to 0x07FF for them.35 The SSM allocates the other identifiers:37

    SE identifier Type Use
    0x0000–0x07FF APID Spacecraft application processes
    0x0800 Router Reserved (spacecraft identifier in the MIB)
    0x1000–0x1FFF – SCOS-2 tasks
    0x2000–0x2FFF – EMCS clients (for example TOPE)
    0x4000–0x4FFF – Ground station application processes
    0x8000–0x87FF APID Alternative routing of spacecraft application processes via "EGSE_8000"
    0x8000–0x8FFF – SCOE application processes
    0x8800 Router Alternative routing of spacecraft application processes via the TC front end
    0x8801 APID TC front end controller
    0x8810–0x8817 APID TM front end, virtual channels 0 to 7
    0x8818 APID TM front end controller
    0xF000–0xFFFF – EGSE gateway and router tasks

    Decimal values in the source

    The manual prints the decimal equivalent of 0x1000 as 4099 and the SCOE range 0x8000–0x8FFF as 34816–36863. The hexadecimal values above are given as printed; 0x1000 is 4096 and 0x8000 is 32768 in decimal (the manual's own Appendix B uses 0x8000 = 32768 as its example). The printed decimal range 34816–36863 corresponds to 0x8800–0x8FFF instead, so either the hexadecimal start or the decimal start of the SCOE range is a misprint; the sources do not say which.3767

    The SE type tells the kernel what it can do with an element. Elements of type APID process requests, so activities (telecommands, procedures, stimuli) can be attached to them. Elements of type Router only carry requests and reports onward. SCOS-2000 was changed to control any element that processes requests, whether it is on board or on the ground.36 An Oracle-based SSM repository was under development but not yet available in 2004, so users populated the mission data through the SCOS-2000 ASCII files, extended for EGSE needs.37

    The EMCS kernel and the SCOE¶

    The kernel is SCOS-2000 with three additions: it can route commands to the spacecraft through the ground station or through a SCOE, it can monitor and control ground processes such as SCOE applications, and it can send commands and process telemetry that are not predefined in the database.38 It connects to the SCOEs over TCP/IP.40

    For PROBA, ESTEC built the EGSE router, a generic facility on Windows for integrating and remotely controlling SCOE equipment. The router provides location transparency, client management with several clients per connection, data transparency, and message delivery either by asynchronous call-back or by synchronous polling. It was written in Delphi as an ActiveX server using Microsoft DCOM, usable from any ActiveX language (Delphi, C++, LabView, Java). Systems without DCOM, such as SCOS-2000, connect through a TCP/IP gateway.41 The Windows TM front end extracts, archives and distributes CCSDS telemetry packets from a bit stream, either from an on-board data handling interface simulator or from baseband equipment. The TC front end turns command requests into a CCSDS stream, using the commercial TC Encoder Shell for the full ground-space protocol.42 For Herschel/Planck the Windows router and front ends were not used. SRON developed a Linux router for Herschel, compatible with the ESTEC set-up on the SCOS side but not on the SCOE side.40 A packet router ICD written at SRON in 2001 is summarised below; that it specifies this Linux router is this wiki's reading, since the ICD names Java but no operating system.117

    EGSE message format¶

    Messages between ground applications on the EGSE router share one header, defined in the EGSE Gateway ICD:56

    Field Size Meaning
    DstID 2 octets Unique identifier of the destination ground application
    SrcID 2 octets Unique identifier of the sending application
    Token 4 octets Unique message identifier, used in reports about the processing of the message
    Time 8 octets SunOS time format: 4 octets of seconds since 1970-01-01T00:00:00 and 4 octets of remaining microseconds
    Msg_Type 1 octet Template number that defines the application data layout
    Spare 1 octet 0
    S/C id 2 octets Spacecraft identifier; 0x66 for PROBA
    Application data any Layout given by the template

    The field sizes and meanings come from §7.1 of the EMCS manual.56 The message types are:56

    Msg_Type Template Direction (from the manual's descriptions)
    1 SCOE command SCOS-2000 to a SCOE
    2 SCOE command verification report SCOE to SCOS-2000
    3 SCOE observation report SCOE to SCOS-2000
    4 Sending TC packet request SCOS-2000 to the TC front end
    5 Sending TC packet verification report TC front end to SCOS-2000
    6 TM packet report TM front end to SCOS-2000
    7 TM frame reports (application data not defined in the manual)

    Template 1 (SCOE command) keeps the PUS application data but replaces the CCSDS and PUS headers by the EGSE header. The parameter structure follows the PUS service type and subtype and is defined in the MIB.58

    Template 2 (SCOE command verification report) carries service type 1 with a subtype from 1 to 8, the 4-octet token of the SCOE command, a step number (for subtypes 5 and 6 only; its size is not printed), a 1-octet code and parameters. Footnotes in the manual say that neither step-level progress reporting nor the code was supported by the EMCS server in 2004. The subtypes are 1 successful acceptance, 2 failed acceptance, 3 successful start, 4 failed start, 5 successful progress, 6 failed progress, 7 successful completion and 8 failed completion, mirroring ST[01].59

    Template 3 (SCOE observation report) lets an EGSE application send any feedback to SCOS, in a structure modelled on a PUS telemetry packet: service type, subtype and a parameter structure like PUS source data. It is sent with the router's "smData" service.60

    Template 4 (sending TC packet) asks the TC front end to uplink a complete CCSDS/PUS TC packet:61

    In BD mode, the UL_Ack report says whether the packet was completely sent to the spacecraft. In AD mode, it says whether the packet was confirmed by the COP-1 protocol through the CLCW. In 2004 the mapping from service type and subtype to MAPID was hard-coded in the SCOS-to-SCOE interface, as it had been for PROBA.61 See TC Space Data Link Protocol and COP-1 for AD and BD frames, MAP IDs and the CLCW.

    Template 5 (sending TC packet verification report) returns service type 1 with subtypes 9 to 14: 9 successful GS, 10 failed GS, 11 successful UL, 12 failed UL, 13 successful on-board acceptance, 14 failed on-board acceptance. It also carries the 4-octet EGSE command token, a code and parameters. SCOS-2000 injects these reports as telemetry packets.62

    Template 6 (TM packet report) wraps a full CCSDS/PUS telemetry packet received by the TM front end. Service 1 reports inside it are injected with a packet ID known to the SCOS-2000 verifier.63

    SPID values for injected verification reports

    The manual gives the packet IDs used to inject Template 5 and service 1 reports as 10005 and 10006 in §7.7 and §7.8, but lists the defaults of the corresponding configuration variables (EGSE_VER_TC_GS_PKTID, EGSE_VER_TC_REP_PKTID) as 23 and 24 in §4.2.626345 The sources do not explain the difference; the values are configurable in any case.

    The acknowledgement policy¶

    PUS defines four acknowledgements that a command can request from the on-board application process: acceptance, start, progress and completion. The EMCS extends this so that a command can be followed through the EMCS kernel, NCTRS and the SCOEs as well. Any element that processes requests, on board or on the ground, may report with the four PUS acknowledgements (Template 2 for SCOE commands, Template 6 for TC packets). Three further acknowledgement requests, with six reports (service 1 subtypes 9 to 14), follow the ground front ends:57

    Acknowledgement Issued by When
    GS_Ack Ground station or SCOE TC front end The telecommand is received
    UL_Ack Ground station or SCOE TC front end A BD-mode telecommand has been sent to the spacecraft
    ONB_Ack Ground station or SCOE TC front end The spacecraft confirms reception of an AD-mode telecommand

The External Command Handler (ECH) always confirms receipt, the PTV reports depend on the MIB and the TC SPACON settings, the ground reports are always delivered, and the on-board reports depend on the application process. The manual warns that the order of the reports cannot be guaranteed. SCOS-2000 can also verify execution through telemetry conditions (the CEV, confirmation of execution verification) instead of, or in addition to, service 1 reports.57

    Internal archives and packet identification¶

    The SCOS EGSE stores every received message (Templates 2, 3, 5 and 6) as an internal SCOS packet with a SPID. SPIDs 0 to 100 are reserved for kernel needs (§4.1.1 says 1 to 100) and can be reallocated through the configuration and the minimum EGSE data set.6443 The SPID is chosen from the template number, the APID, the service type and subtype, and the PI1 and PI2 identification fields, as for spacecraft packets.64 Specific processing then applies:65

    • Time stamping depends on EGSE_TM_FILING_TIME_TYPE: 1 uses the reception time on the SCOS machine, 2 uses the time field of the router header, and 3 converts the on-board time in the packet through time correlation, with a sanity check. A packet whose on-board time differs from the current time by more than EGSE_TM_OBT_THRESHOLD gets the current time instead, a warning, and the type "PR" rather than "PG" in the packet history.
    • Checksum verification: packets that fail can be discarded, or archived in a dedicated data stream and flagged invalid together with all their parameters.
    • Request verification: Template 2, Template 5 and service 1 reports go to the SCOS-2000 verifier and are stored under system SPIDs.
    • Unknown messages: an observation report or TM packet without a matching pid entry is "rejected" and filed under a wrapper SPID. After the MIB is updated and re-imported, the PDS_DISP task copies the stored messages into their proper SPID archives.66

    The minimum EGSE data set¶

    The EGSE needs some generic MIB definitions to work. They are delivered as MIN_EGSE_v2, and every name in it starts with the letter "Z" so that it is easy to recognise.43 A mission adapts the TC packet header (tcp, pcdf, pcpc), the time-tag wrapper command ZLOADTT and the binary command ZBINTC (ccf, cdf, cpc), the definitions that decode rejected and service 1 packets (dpc, pcf, plf), and the version entry (vdf).43 Two scripts manage databases: mergeDATA merges several data sets (for example MIN_EGSE and one instrument) into a new set, and setSCOSdata points SCOS-2000 at a given MIB, links its TOPE procedures, installs its archive and cache configuration, and runs the import.44 See the MIB for the tables and MIB tooling for editors.

    Command routing in the MIB: the dst table¶

    The MIB table dst routes commands by APID. The ICD says it is optional and only used by SCOS-2000-based EGSE systems. Each route is a string of up to three dot-separated fields: the destination system (NCTRS, the default, or EGSE), the request type (SCOE for non-spacecraft commands, TC for spacecraft commands) and, for spacecraft commands not sent through NCTRS, the address of the ground equipment that sends them. The ICD's example EGSE.TC.0x8800 sends spacecraft commands through the SCOE and the TC front end 0x8800.112 This matches the system element 0x8800 of the EMCS manual.37

All 91 commands on APID 1372 have names starting with ZIC, and 7 of their descriptions mention the SPICE instrument; for example ZIC00085 is described as opening the door of the SPICE optics unit.113114

    TOPE: the Test and Operations Procedure Environment¶

    ECSS-E-70-32 specifies a common procedure language for test and operations, PLUTO (Procedure Language for Users in Test and Operations; see ECSS-E-ST-70-32C). When PLUTO tools were not yet available, ESA delivered to the Herschel/Planck instrument teams a procedure environment based on Tcl/Tk, called TOPE (Test and Operations Procedure Environment). TOPE starts commands to the spacecraft or the SCOEs through the EMCS kernel, follows their execution through the reports that SCOS returns, and monitors the space system through telemetry and ground parameters.39 Procedures may use any low-level Tcl/Tk function. The constructs defined since issue 3.0 of the manual are not compatible with the earlier TOPE language.46 Later sources expand the acronym differently: GMV writes "Test procedure, preparation and execution" and Terma "Test & Operations Procedure Executive".10592

    Running TOPE¶

    In the PACS instrument test set-up of 2004, the router and EGSE gateway were started first, then a gateway for the PACS test equipment interface, then SCOS-2000 from its task launcher (server tasks, then client applications), and finally the EXIF tasks and a TOPE instance from a second launcher. Starting a second procedure in the same TOPE window suspended the first until the second finished. Parallel procedures needed another TOPE window and another EXIF_CHx task.7071 The manual's debugger, TUBA, could step through procedures but had known limits, such as treating several procedures as one sequential flow.68

    The ILT packet router¶

    The router at the centre of that diagram is specified in the Herschel EGSE Packet Router ICD (SRON-G/HIFI/ICD/2001-001, issue 1.1 of 23 October 2001, prepared by A. de Jonge and agreed by the EGSE and ICC managers of HIFI, PACS and SPIRE and by the HSC). It distributes PS-ICD telemetry and telecommand packets among SCOS-2000, the test equipment interface computers, the HCSS telemetry ingester and the CDMS simulator during instrument level tests, and the same interface was to serve the HCSS ingestion of raw telemetry in later phases. It runs on any platform with Java and listens on one agreed TCP port.115116117

    Every message is a 1-octet messageType, a 4-octet contentLength (at most 1100, TBC) and the content; integers are most significant octet first.120 Twelve message types use three content structures:120122

    Type Mnemonic Sent by Content
    1 USER_DATA Client or router One complete PS-ICD packet; contentLength = packet length field + 7
    2, 3 ADD_CLIENT, DEL_CLIENT Client Subscribe to, or unsubscribe from, a packet address
    4, 5 ASK_CLIENT, SHOW_CLIENT Client, router List the subscriptions (with IP address and port, for debugging)
    6 NAME_CLIENT Client Must be the first message; a unique client name
    7, 8 ADD_BLOCK, DEL_BLOCK Client Block, or unblock, a route given by source name, destination name and packet address
    9, 10 ASK_BLOCK, SHOW_BLOCK Client, router List the blocking table
    11, 12 ASK_TRAFFIC, SHOW_TRAFFIC Client, router Packet counts per address, source and destination

    The packet address is the APID for telemetry and 4096 plus the APID for telecommands, that is the type bit and the APID taken from the 16-bit packet ID (the ICD calls this "masking out the Type and APID fields"); the value 8192 is reserved: it is the wild card of blocking entries and marks "no address" in list replies. The router never looks inside the packet. It forwards a copy of each USER_DATA message to every client that subscribed to its address, unless a blocking entry matches, an entry matching when each of its source name, destination name and address is equal to the message's or empty (address 8192). Replies to ASK_* messages count down in a messageSequenceNumber to 0.121123124

    Worked example (illustrative values): one telecommand through the router

    A HIFI telecommand to APID 1024 (0x400) starts with the packet ID 0x1C00 (version 0b000, type 1, data field header flag 1). Masking out version and header flag leaves 0x1400 = 4096 + 1024 = 5120, the packet address to which the instrument simulator subscribes. A packet whose length field is 17 (a 24-octet packet) travels as USER_DATA with contentLength 24 = 17 + 7: the header octets are 01 00 00 00 18. The packet values are chosen for illustration; the rules are those of the ICD.121120144

    There is no handshake, flow control or security: TCP provides the integrity, unauthorised access must be prevented by an isolated network, and after a router or client crash the client simply reconnects with the same name and repeats its subscriptions, packets in transit being lost.     Inference

    PIPE's acknowledgements play some of the roles of the EMCS ground acknowledgements described above: the ACKTC resembles the GS_Ack (the front end has accepted the command), and the TC report in AD mode, sent after the CLCW check, resembles the ONB_Ack. The TC echo, a decoded copy of the transmitted CLTU that the DFE also produces for commands arriving from the MOC through the NDIU and that the CCS forwards to the instrument stations, has no EMCS counterpart.     The HPCCS and its database¶

    The Herschel/Planck Central Checkout System (HPCCS) that resulted was developed by Terma (the Netherlands) under an Alcatel Alenia Space Italia subcontract, and is based on SCOS-2000 with additional test functions. The Herschel/Planck System DataBase (HPSDB) was developed by GMV.8283 NLR's account of the attitude control subsystem shows how data flowed into it. The subsystem's test equipment combined the EuroSim real-time simulator with the HPCCS, which commanded and monitored EuroSim through remote messages and commands in the same format as spacecraft TM/TC. A dedicated database therefore had to generate the SCOS-2000 input files. The available SCOS-2000 MS Access database did not fit: it supported a single mission, not two missions with common data, and offered no configuration control at object level. Dutch Space built its operations database in the DOORS requirements tool instead, and exported to the HPSDB, from which SCOS-2000 files were produced for AIT and operations.83 See MIB tooling.

    Terma's CCS External ICD (H-P-4-TE-ID-8020, issue 1.8 of 18 January 2006; issue 1.0 was prepared for the CCS PDR in May 2002) names the checkout system's external interfaces: PIPE on the SCOE/DFE LAN (which also carries the TM dumps of the front end) and on the instrument station LAN, a World Wide Web station, and two file interfaces with the HPSDB. Bridge files bring the MIB into the CCS, in the format of the SCOS-2000 import ICD issue 5.1 with explicit exceptions; update files go back, one tab-separated line per telecommand or parameter that a test sequence has validated (time, c or p, name, test sequence name and issue, host), for example 2002.123.20.16.32.43.123 c BATTERY03_ON battest1 12 hp0-s, printed in the ICD with one time field more than its stated format YYYY.DDD.HH.MM.SS.mmm.145146147 The exceptions turn the MIB into a checkout database: a parameter beyond its danger limits starts a test sequence named danger_<parname>, ocp gains delta limits, new tables add calibration selection (cur), parameter and packet groups (grp, grpa, grpk), the equipment list (sco, with host and port) and the distribution of telecommands by APID (tcd) and of telemetry by SPID (tmd) to that equipment, parameter names starting with YZ are reserved for CCS system parameters, and SPIDs 100 to 999 for internal EGSE use, next to the SPIDs up to 100 that the instrument EGSE reserved for its own kernel.14814915015115243 The full list, with the identifier widenings of ECP 1, the logarithmic curves of ECP 3 and the default sub-schedules, is analysed on Mission tailoring of the MIB. The system EGSE specification also required the CCS to host an HPSDB mirror site; GMV's user manual describes such sites as able to change only real (instantiated) objects, with data exchanged with the central site as XML files that the managers carry by hand.13153

    The routing tables were filled. A Herschel release note of April 2008 prints the tcd configuration of the flight model database: APIDs 0, 16, 512, 1024, 1152 and 1280 (the CPDU commands, the CDMS, the ACMS and the three instruments) go to TMTCDFE, and the EGSE APIDs to their own equipment, for example 2017 to CDMUSCOE, 2026 to PFM_CRYO and 2042 to 2044 to HHIFIEGSE, HPACSEGSE and HSPIREEGSE. Its tmd configuration sends HIFI, PACS and SPIRE monitoring to the matching instrument EGSE, and CCU and cryo SCOE monitoring to all three.154

    Two ways to route a command

    The CCS routes by tcd (APID to an equipment name) and sco (equipment name to host and port). The standard MIB's dst table instead gives each APID a route string such as EGSE.TC.0x8800, naming the destination system, the request type and the address of the front end (EMCS system element 0x8800).152112 Neither scheme appears in the other system's documentation, and sco, tcd and tmd did not enter the standard import ICD.155

    Commercial checkout systems around SCOS-2000¶

    Terma: from SCOS-2000-based CCS to compatible re-implementations¶

    Terma delivered several system-level checkout systems for ESA missions with a CCS based on SCOS-2000, and reused them at instrument level.84 Its 2010 paper explains why it then built a new small system, CMDVS (Commanding Monitoring Data-handling & Visualization System), with Satellite Services BV (SSBV). A SCOS-2000-based system needs a separate EGSE database for the front ends. Commanding front ends with TM and TC packets is awkward and limited by the speed of the SCOS-2000 telecommand chain. Maintaining SCOS-2000 on modern platforms was getting more expensive. Developing a monitoring and control system from scratch had cost about 30 MEUR in the past, and users would not accept a completely new system.8485

    The answer was compatibility. CMDVS keeps the CCS test language and the SCOS-2000 MIB interface, which Terma describes as "not perfectly normalized" but well known and well understood.85 By early 2010 it implemented all SCOS-2000 TM and command processing features. Front ends are driven through plugins that expose their APIs directly to the test language instead of through TM/TC packets. Variable packets are fully processed, header generation is fully database driven, all PUS type codes up to 64 bits are supported, and up to 64 verification stages can be configured (SCOS-2000 has no stage after the last completion report).8586 The same plugin technique was moved back into Terma's SCOS-2000-based CCS, whose external interface manager became a generic process with project-specific plugins.87

    Terma's current products, CCS5 (multi-user checkout), SCS5 (multi-user operations) and TSC (single user), are full new implementations that keep the SCOS-2000 MIB data model.8897 Terma's 2024 presentation explains the decision. Legacy systems such as SCOS-2000 were becoming uneconomic to maintain, too slow and inflexible for new missions, and onerous to install. Asking users to adopt a completely new data model would require retraining, and Terma was too small to impose industry-wide standards.88

    Translating SCOS-2000 databases to other checkout systems¶

    Not every prime contractor used a SCOS-2000-based checkout system. Thales Alenia Space Italia (TAS-I) and Intecs built the SRDB Translator to import a SCOS-2000 MIB into the database of TAS-I's ECHO checkout system. Their paper notes that SCOS-2000 had become a standard for ESA mission control and, in most cases, for EGSE too, so the Satellite Reference Database (SRDB) was almost always based on the SCOS-2000 run-time database.98 The two databases cover similar entities, but a one-to-one table mapping was "almost inapplicable". Each item falls into one of three cases: present in both (converted), present only in the SRDB (handled, ignored or added to ECHO), or needed only by ECHO (supplied separately).99 Examples of the conversion rules:100

    From TOPE to EGS-CC¶

    ESA uses two flavours of SCOS: SCOS-2000 for operations at ESOC and SCOS-EGSE for EGSE applications at ESTEC, which includes TOPE. With EGS-CC replacing both, repositories of TOPE procedures would in principle have to be rewritten in the EGS-CC automation language.104 GMV and ESA instead connected TOPE to EGS-CC in the ERFEC activity (EGSE reference facility in EGS-CC context), for the Real-Time Bench of ESTEC's Avionics Test Bench (ATB-RTB).104108

    In the ATB-RTB, TOPE runs inside a test sequencing subsystem with four parts besides EXIF:106

    TPREP (TOPE Preparation Tools) Editor and consistency checker for test sequences

    The TOPE Adaptation Layer (TAL) replaces EXIF. It implements CORBA servers that comply with the existing TSEQ/SCOS-2000 IDL files, so TSEQ runs unmodified. Inside EGS-CC it uses the monitoring and control access, definition management, messaging and configuration services.108 EGS-CC models the space system as a Monitoring and Control Model (MCM): a hierarchy of monitoring and control elements with activities, events and parameters, independent of TM/TC packets.107 At start-up, TAL reads the ASCII MIB files and a mapping file, generates the EGS-CC conceptual data model from the MIB, and EGS-CC loads it into the MCM. TAL then translates TOPE's commands, parameters and events to MCM activities, parameters and events, and back.109 The approach has two constraints: the ASCII MIB must always be present (the ATB used the EagleEye mission MIB), and new TM/TC defined in the MCM cannot be written back to the MIB.109 TAL is written in Java with JacORB and deployed as OSGi bundles inside an EGS-CC session.110 See ancillary systems, MICONYS and EGS-CC.

    The system database faced the same migration. At SESP 2015 Thales Alenia Space presented the System Reference Data Base (SRDB), its adaptation of the Herschel/Planck System Database for the MTG programme. (The acronym is the same as that of the Satellite Reference Database read by the SRDB Translator above; whether the two databases are related is not stated in the available sources.) The SRDB keeps the HPSDB concepts (objects, items and pseudo-items with category flags, physical and logical instantiation, working, reference and archive areas), adds the attachment of former object versions, and was to map its high-level data model onto the EGS-CC product structure and its items onto the other EGS-CC packages, which in 2015 was "still to be done".156157158 Where TAL reads an ASCII MIB at start-up and cannot write new definitions back, the SRDB plan puts the reference database upstream of EGS-CC.109

    Inference

    Contrasting the two approaches (a MIB converted at run time by TAL, a reference database mapped to the EGS-CC model) is this wiki's reading; the two papers do not refer to each other, and whether the SRDB mapping was completed is not stated in the available sources.
