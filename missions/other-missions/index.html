    Other missions and SCOS-2000 users¶

    Most of what this wiki knows about SCOS-2000 and the MIB comes from three mission documentation sets: Solar Orbiter, Herschel and Planck and Rosetta, Mars Express and Venus Express. Many more missions appear in the library sources, usually in passing: in ESA's own lists of SCOS-2000 users, in conference papers on the mission-family approach, in papers on test tools and database converters, and in recent papers on payload commanding and on the transition to EGS-CC. This page collects those statements, mission by mission, and says where each one comes from. It does not add facts from memory: when a source is silent about something (launch dates, current status, the content of a mission's database), the page says so.

    How to read this page

    Mission lists from 2000 and 2003 describe plans at the time ("development ongoing", "in evaluation"); they are historical statements, not confirmations of what was finally built. No database (MIB) of any mission on this page is in the library; only the Solar Orbiter MIB is publicly available as data, and the queries quoted below run on it.

    Before SCOS-2000: four generations of control systems¶

    ESOC decided in 1974 to develop a reusable mission control infrastructure. The first generation, the Multi Mission Support System (MSSS; "Multi-Satellite Support System" in the 2007 ESA Bulletin), entered service in 1977 and, when the SCOS-II paper was written, was still used by the MARECS and ECS missions; the second, SCOS-I, followed in 1989 and supported Hipparcos, ERS-1 and EURECA.125 The third, SCOS-II, was a distributed client-server system on Unix workstations; its first client missions were to be Huygens (1997), Artemis (1997) and Envisat (1998).12 SCOS-2000 then replaced SCOS-II.

    timeline
  title ESOC mission control infrastructure (as stated by the sources)
  1977 : MSSS enters service (MARECS, ECS)
  1989 : SCOS-I (Hipparcos, ERS-1, EURECA) - packet telemetry, no generic TC chain
  1995-1999 : SCOS-II - distributed, C++/UNIX, generic TC chain
  1997 : First SCOS(-2) missions - Huygens, Meteosat MTP LEOP, Teamsat
  1999 : SCOS-2000 R2.0 (INTEGRAL SVTs)
  2000 : R2.1 (MSG SVTs) : R2.2 planned for September
  2003 : R3.0 (classic and evolution lines merged) : R3.1 planned for September
  2004 : R4.0 planned - multi-mission, full EGSE compliance

    The sources do not agree on every date. Peccia's 2003 slides give MSSS as 1984-1996, SCOS-I "since 1989" (packet telemetry, no generic telecommand chain) and SCOS-II as 1995-1999, "superceded by SCOS-2000"; the ESA Bulletin of 2001 says work on SCOS started around 1992 and supported its first missions - Huygens, the Meteosat (MTP) LEOP and Teamsat - in 1997, and the 2000 presentation names the product SCOS-2 up to release R1.4 (until 1998) and SCOS-2000 from release R2.0 (1999).3910 The release dates in the timeline come from the 2000 presentation of April 2000 (R2.0 September 1999 for the INTEGRAL system validation tests, R2.1 March 2000 for MSG, R2.2 planned for September 2000) and from the slides of March 2003 (R3.0 January 2003, R3.1 planned for September 2003, R4.0 planned for June 2004).114 The release history as a whole, including the later issues of the Database Import ICD that name R2.2 and R4.0, is on SCOS-2000; the parallel history of ESA's packet standards is on From ESA PSS to CCSDS and ECSS.

    Missions in more detail¶

    INTEGRAL¶

    INTEGRAL is the first mission the sources tie to a specific SCOS-2000 release: R2.0 of September 1999 was the release for the INTEGRAL system validation tests.11 Peccia's slide of an SCOS-2000-based MCS in its ground-segment context uses INTEGRAL as the example: an INTEGRAL monitoring and control system (IMCS) fed by an operations database (ODB) built from the satellite database import, an INTEGRAL overall simulator, a mission planning system, the flight dynamics system, an off-line evaluation system and long history file, and an INTEGRAL Security Distribution System delivering data and CD-ROMs to the INTEGRAL Science Operations Centre (ISOC) and the INTEGRAL Science Data Centre (ISDC).8

    INTEGRAL also left two tools behind. Its MS Access database editor, "compatible with SCOS 2000", was offered to the FIRST/Planck teams in 2000 as a database option.1413 The HIFI team evaluated it in April 2001 as the SCOS-2000 Database Editing Subsystem, version 2.0, "also referred to as the Integral MS Access editor"; the developer's answers in that report explain INTEGRAL's choices, for example keys on the first six fields because of the structure of the incoming satellite database, commands with more than 200 parameters, and consistency checking planned in a separate application.17 On the science side, the ISDC in Geneva reused software it had developed for INTEGRAL to build the Planck LFI telemetry handler and quick-look displays.18 MIB tooling covers database editors in general; INTEGRAL's flight procedures and configuration control are on operations preparation.

    XMM-Newton, Envisat and ERS: migrations from SCOS-1¶

    The 2000 presentation lists Envisat and XMM (MOC and SOC) as future migrations from SCOS-1, and ERS as a migration with a first delivery at the end of 1999.12 The 2005 GSAW slides cite software that started as mission developments and was reused widely: the File Transfer System from XMM and the Mission Planning System from Envisat (and database editors from CryoSat).19 XMM also shaped the Herschel/Planck requirements: the annex on the satellite users manual of the Herschel/Planck OIRD "is derived directly from the example of the XMM mission".23 The SCOS-II paper also names Envisat as a 1998 client of that earlier system.2

    PROBA¶

    PROBA, a technology satellite with a 32-bit processor, an Earth-observation imager and experiments in on-board autonomy and planning, used a 2-m antenna at Redu independent of the ESA network.15 Its common EGSE and mission control system was built on SCOS 2 with an ORACLE database extended to the SCOE equipment, a router written in Delphi, LabVIEW applications and a Tcl/Tk procedure language; the router and Tcl/Tk tool were then offered to the FIRST/Planck teams.1513 The EGSE & Mission Control System (EMCS) delivered to the Herschel/Planck instrument teams states that it is "based on the PROBA approach, but uses SCOS 2000 in place of SCOS 2", and that the MS-Windows EGSE router developed by ESTEC for PROBA was not used on Herschel/Planck.16 See SCOS-2000 as EGSE.

    SMART-1¶

    SMART-1 appears in both client lists (an experimental propulsion mission due for launch in 2003 in the 2003 list) and, with Rosetta and Mars Express, among the planetary missions for which ESA decided early to archive data in NASA's Planetary Data System (PDS) format.12524

    The Earth Explorer family: CryoSat, GOCE, Aeolus and Swarm¶

    The Earth Explorer missions form ESOC's Earth observation family: low orbits (250-800 km), often polar, with about 15 short passes of about 10 minutes a day over a single station, and very similar operational concepts despite different platforms.26 Their on-board time management illustrates the differences: CryoSat synchronises its time via the French DORIS payload and ground beacons, GOCE uses an on-board counter kept in step by the ground, and Aeolus has a GPS receiver in addition to its counter.26 The 2007 article records that CryoSat, the first Earth Explorer Opportunity mission, was lost in a launch failure in October 2005, that GOCE was foreseen for launch at the end of 2007 and Aeolus in 2008, and lists CryoSat-2, Swarm, EarthCARE and the GMES Sentinels as future members.26

    For this family ESOC used a three-layer delta approach, inserting an Earth Explorer Mission Kernel between SCOS-2000 and each mission.27 CryoSat was the first mission specified as a delta against SCOS-2000, GOCE as a delta against CryoSat (which defined the kernel in practice), and Aeolus as a delta against the kernel.20

    Requirements at definition phase Kernel Mission-specific Total
    CryoSat SRD 406 35 441
    GOCE SRD 129 62 191

    The table reproduces the counts on the GSAW 2005 slide.21 The same slides list what each mission contributed to the kernel: CryoSat brought an off-line database editor and consistency checker, command and parameter identifier extensions, playback telemetry processing with command verification, a mission planning system, the file transfer system, handling of bad time quality per virtual channel, configurable on-board software maintenance, on-board queue dump processing, XML-based ground interface files and the prime/backup real-time plus off-line archive server architecture; GOCE added planning enhancements, telemetry gap identification, task parameter files with microsecond times, automatic telemetry replay and a time correlator; Aeolus added weekly playback consolidation, OBSM enhancements, orbital coordinates and automatic NIS schedule execution.22

    CryoSat also changed SCOS-2000 itself. In 2002 ESOC reported that CryoSat had implemented a range of SPIDs for service 1 report packets (Rosetta had allowed any SPID), to be integrated in R3.1.29 The MIB ICD records a "Cryosat special command/sequence parameter types repatriation" in ICD version 6.0: command or sequence parameters of type Command ID (encoded automatically as the raw data of another command), of type Parameter ID (encoded as a telemetry parameter's on-board identifier) and a default sub-schedule ID for time-tagged commands in the ccf and csf tables; ICD 6.1 also integrated a sustaining DCR labelled CRY-OG-655 (DCR 3398) that updated the descriptions of PID_PI1_VAL and PID_PI2_VAL - the "CRY" prefix suggests CryoSat, but the annex does not say so.3031

    The three CryoSat features are used by Solar Orbiter today. In the September 2025 release, 2 command parameters have CPC_CATEG = A (Command ID: "Complete TC packet" and "TC to send upon event"), used for example by ZCD00B04 "Insert Telecommands in Command Schedule", TC(11,4), and by event-action definitions such as ZCS01301, TC(19,1); 152 parameters have CPC_CATEG = P (Parameter ID); and 5 304 of 5 454 commands and all 3 404 sequences give a sub-schedule ID.343233

    Swarm, a constellation of three Earth magnetic field satellites launched in 2013, is the first mission ESOC is migrating to an EGS-CC-based MCS (see the last section).59

    Galileo¶

    Peccia's 2003 list has a Galileo MCS for 34 satellites "in evaluation".5 Two later papers show Galileo using SCOS-2000-format databases in testing. Thales Alenia Space Italia and Intecs wrote the first version of their SRDB Translator for the Galileo GIOVE-B project, whose satellite reference database was a mission-tailored SCOS-2000 database delivered as a single MS Access 2000 file and validated with TAS-I's own ECHO checkout system.36 Terma's CMDVS monitoring and control system, compatible with the SCOS-2000 database and test language, was used at payload or instrument level on Galileo FOC, and a Galileo FOC thermal monitoring SCOE ran it on a small low-power PC.3942

    Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, EarthCARE and SGEO¶

    The Comi paper explains why converters exist at all: SCOS-2000 had become a standard for ESA mission control centres and in most cases for EGSE, so satellite reference databases were "almost always" based on the SCOS-2000 run-time database.35 For Sentinel-1, where TAS-I validated at satellite level and a SCOS-2000 database was an imposed requirement, an enhanced SRDB Translator let the AIV team keep using ECHO.3637 Terma reports CMDVS at payload or instrument level on Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, EarthCARE and SGEO (and BepiColombo and Galileo FOC).39 A small trace of Sentinel-2 reached Solar Orbiter: issue 2 of the Solar Orbiter packet structure ICD updated its APID table "based on SW donor being Sentinel 2".43

    Terma's paper also gives numbers that matter for anyone building on SCOS-2000 as EGSE: the SCOS-2000 telecommand chain handled 2 packets per second with checking or 20 without; CMDVS supports all SCOS-2000 verification stages plus a finalisation stage, with up to 64 stages configurable by mask; and the MIB interface was described as "not perfectly normalized" but well known and understood.4041

    BepiColombo¶

    In 2007 ESOC expected BepiColombo (then due for launch to Mercury in 2013) and Solar Orbiter (2015) to join the interplanetary family of Rosetta, Mars Express and Venus Express.28 A 2025 paper gives the launch as October 2018, in cooperation with JAXA, followed by a 7.2-year cruise with nine planetary flybys.50 The documents in this library show how closely BepiColombo and Solar Orbiter are tied on the ground:

    • the Solar Orbiter Data Delivery ICD was tidied up and aligned "with the BepiColombo DDID" in its issue 1 revision 2, refers time correlation to a BepiColombo technical note, lists a "(Solar Orbiter/BepiColombo) Packetiser ICD" and the "BepiColombo and Solar-Orbiter MCS SRS" among its documents, and says that the two missions share the EDDS backup server;454647
    • the Solar Orbiter packet structure ICD, in the definition of the telecommand acknowledgement flags, still reads "The bit settings defined for BepiColombo are as follows";44
    • TAS-I used the Sentinel-1 version of its SRDB Translator on BepiColombo to import SCOS-2000 databases for on-board computer and mass-memory tests; those databases followed an earlier ICD issue and needed added columns before import. The paper's metrics for the two databases are:38
    BepiColombo database OBC SSMM
    Approximate number of records 10 000 10 000
    Parameters 3 745 1 947
    TM packets 521 1 921
    TM samples 2 944 2 540

    Inference

    The leftover BepiColombo sentence in the Solar Orbiter ICD and the shared packetiser ICD suggest that the Solar Orbiter TM/TC definitions were derived from BepiColombo's. The sources state the shared documents, not the derivation.

    During the cruise, BepiColombo instrument teams send their commanding requests to the MOC as Command Request Files (CRFs): Payload Operations Requests (PORs) and Payload Direct Operations Requests (PDORs). Since launch more than about 1 500 of them were produced with SPOT Cruise, a web tool built at ESAC that checks each request against the applicable MIB, handles MIB updates and conflicts, and delivers the CRF to ESOC's mission planning and control systems.5051 The paper expands MIB as "Mission Information Database" and explains the motivation: before this centralised framework, users' own CRF tools often clashed with the MIB and the ESOC ICDs, causing long exchanges with the flight control team.49 When ESOC started developing its EGS-CC based systems, the BepiColombo simulator was the mission simulator used for parallel test sessions.55

    JUICE¶

    JUICE, launched in April 2023 and due at Jupiter in 2031, adapted the BepiColombo CRF editor in 2021 as SPOT JUICE, without the 3D flyby visualisation, procedure builder and simulation functions; it supported payload ground testing, near-Earth commissioning, six-monthly check-outs and the Lunar-Earth gravity assist of August 2024, with more than about 700 requests.52 Hera and Ariel have shown interest in the framework.53 In 2019 the JUICE MCS (J-MCS) was "the first target mission control system to be developed based on EGOS-CC", with a pilot to demonstrate maturity before a commitment due in September 2019.54 Whether that commitment was confirmed for JUICE is not stated in the available sources; the 2025 SpaceOps paper names Swarm and Ariel as the first EGS-CC missions.59

    Solar Orbiter¶

    Solar Orbiter has its own pages: Solar Orbiter, the Solar Orbiter MIB, its use of PUS and the SWA instrument database.

    The EGS-CC era¶

    For decades ESOC has monitored and controlled satellites with SCOS-2000 and complementary products forming the MICONYS (MIssion CONtrol sYStem) suite; the 2025 SpaceOps paper states that "the signs of obsolescence of SCOS-2000 based solutions can no longer be ignored".56 In April 2024 ESOC re-confirmed that the next-generation MCS will be based on the European Ground Systems Common Core (EGS-CC); the transition is predicted to take at least 7 years from the first operational use of EGS-CC (in 2026), which puts the end of life of SCOS no earlier than 2033, driven by the number of missions involved (above 40).5758 Swarm was chosen as the pilot for migrating a flying mission at no risk, with SCOS-2000 still available in parallel; Ariel (first SVTs in 2027, launch in 2029) is to be the first mission prepared for launch on the new system.59

    The migration of tailoring data is planned in three categories:60

    Category Tailoring data Tools
    1 (e.g. Swarm) Native SCOS-2000 MIB, converted on import into the EGS-CC tailoring data model (TDM) S2K generation tools MOIS, ProToS, MATIS, DABYS, FOPPER; OPEN-M as intermediate; may stay in category 1
    2 (Ariel expected, to be confirmed) Originally an S2K MIB, fully migrated to EGS-CC formats with converters OPEN-M replaces MOIS, MATIS preparation, ProToS, FOPPER, DABYS and other MIB tools
    3 (new missions) Delivered by industry directly in EGS-CC TDM format everything in OPEN-M, also for AIV

    Current work for Swarm covers commanding-chain enhancements (pre-release checks), playback telemetry, SLE link handling, historical data retrieval, a refurbished time correlation and on-board queue modelling; file-based operations, on-board software maintenance, security and PUS-C use cases (with the Generic Operational Interface Requirements) come next, with a fully operational solution expected in 2027.61 The ancillary products and the EGS-CC context are described on Ancillary systems, MICONYS and the move to EGS-CC, and the OPEN preparation environment on operations preparation; how a MIB maps to other data models is discussed on XTCE and ECSS-E-ST-70-31C.

    Mission identifiers in SCOS-2000 headers¶

    The SCOS-2000 packet header that ESOC archives and delivers to users carries a spacecraft ID and an MCS mission ID; for Solar Orbiter both are 650, and the DDID adds that the mission ID should not be used by external users.48 The values used by other missions (for example BepiColombo or JUICE) are not stated in the available sources; this wiki has no library document that lists them.
